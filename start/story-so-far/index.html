    The story so far¶

    Start here · about 12 min · no maths

    In plain words

    We're hunting for planets that orbit two stars at once, using free public data from NASA's TESS telescope. So far we've built a search tool, tested it honestly on a planet that's already known, and chosen 549 two-star systems to search. No new planet yet. On 27 to 28 October we searched all 549 and found nothing that looks like a planet; The first real search explains what that means.

    What this page answers: what happened, in order, and where we stand today. Each day has a longer lesson in the Field notes.

    The idea in one paragraph¶

    Some stars come in pairs, two suns orbiting each other. A planet can orbit the pair, like Tatooine. When it passes in front of one of the suns, that sun dims a little: a transit (Chapter 2). With two moving suns, the transits don't arrive on a timetable (Chapter 5), so the usual search methods miss them. That's our opening. NASA's Kepler telescope found thousands of two-star systems between 2009 and 2013 LIKELY. TESS has been watching many of them again since 2018. A planet whose orbit has slowly tilted into view could be crossing now, in TESS data nobody has searched this way. That campaign is called G1.

    Day 1: Sunday 19 October 2026, the founding¶

    • 25 rounds of questions set the rules: what we hunt, how sure we must be, what you decide and what I decide. They're in RESEARCH-PLAN.md.
    • Research waves. I read what's known: only 14 transiting two-sun planets exist VERIFIED. I measured how big TESS files are on your PC VERIFIED and checked who else searches.
    • G1 chosen. From your phone you said: "your searches are great. Follow it."
    • This book was founded, with a quiet, professional look (your rule: not gamey).
    • Accounts: you made ORCID and ADS accounts, so later we can sign work and read papers through the official index.

    Night 1: 19 to 20 October, the overnight run¶

    1. A better detector. Version 2 of our search re-found the known planet TOI-1338 b in 4 of the 8 sectors where it crossed VERIFIED. It sees planets down to about 6 Earth sizes in one sector VERIFIED. Noise alone still makes fake dips VERIFIED. Chapter 8
    2. A prediction came true. A 2024 paper predicted TOI-1338 b would cross on 16 December 2025. Our search found it that night VERIFIED, about 56 minutes late. That's interesting, but not proof of anything.
    3. Cleaning fakes automatically failed. Rejecting dips near spacecraft events also threw away real ones. So now we flag those dips for a human instead.
    4. The G1 list was frozen: 549 stars from Kepler's catalogue VERIFIED, with fingerprints of the source files.
    5. QLP data was fixed. Half of G1 only has QLP light curves (Chapter 3), and they were full of fake dips from scattered light. The fix borrows NASA's good time windows.
    6. Our first real G1 star, Kepler-16: one dip flagged, and on inspection it was the telescope wobbling before a thruster firing. Nothing survived. Chapter 10
    7. STANLEY (the professionals' search tool, Part 5) was installed and run on TOI-1338. It pointed near the right period, but its scores aren't calibrated yet.

    Morning 20 October¶

    • Compass was born: your review system that checks every big idea before we build it. Its first verdict on our upgrade plans was REVISE.
    • Realistic fake planets finally worked after I found a bug in STANLEY. Our cleaning keeps 90 to 100% of each dip. We find 6-Earth-size planets 8 times in 10, and about 1 crossing in 11 is lost inside the stars' eclipses VERIFIED.
    • NASA's candidate site now wants papers first VERIFIED. That's a decision for you, later.
    • Afternoon: the Bible Overhaul. Writing the labs caught three of our own errors (a noise number, STANLEY's best period, and a hole in the G1 list), all corrected and logged. The field note has the details.
    • Evening: the quality-filter test (a seventh test in Chapter 8). A milder data filter finds the two crossings we couldn't see before, but costs sensitivity elsewhere; and the raw flux gave a quieter noise estimate than the cleaned flux in several sectors. One star only; nothing in the detector changed. Field note.
    • 21 October: the readiness bar. The home page now shows how complete our plan is (36 of 100), built so that every point needs proof. The hunt is on hold until you say start. Field note.
    • 23 October: the book was rebuilt around a map of the real sky, with three tabs, optional labs and a review page for each Part. Nothing about the science changed. Field note.
    • You paused the hunt to start this overhaul: "I can't let an AI do all the work while I just sit and say yes or no."

    What's left before the real hunt¶

    1. Smarter quality flags: warn about scattered light instead of deleting it.
    2. A pixel check: is the dip on our star, or on a neighbour? (Chapter 11)
    3. A bigger fake-planet test (hundreds of planets), so "nothing found" has a measured meaning.
    4. Decide the 136 unmeasured stars: a quarter of the G1 list has no measured binary shape VERIFIED. Your call (decision log).

    5. Run G1: 549 stars, about 6.3 GB of light curves.

    6. 27 to 28 October: the real search. You said start. We froze the settings, searched all 549 stars, found 604 dips, and cut them to 12 with our rules; the upside-down control still found more. Four versions of the filter followed, each one fixing something we learned. The first real search.
