# 30 October 2026: we tried to make the filter better and learned why it will not be free

<p class="ss-meta">Field note · day 12 · about 6 min</p>

!!! plain "In plain words"
    Since 29 October we have been asking one question: how can our filter find smaller circumbinary planets? Today we finished a long round of tests. Every idea we tried either found more fake dips as fast as it found more real ones, or found nothing new. That is a result, not a failure of effort. It tells us the filter is close to a balance: more planets caught costs more false alarms, about one for one. The choice of how many false alarms to accept is yours, not a thing to tune quietly.

## What we did, and why

1. **We asked whether the next big step (stacking) was worth it.** Stacking means adding up several transits of the same planet to see a smaller one. Before doing any work we wrote a rule down: skip it if the known planets are mostly slow and stacking reaches few of our stars. We read the NASA Exoplanet Archive: 14 transiting circumbinary planets are known, 11 of them have periods over 100 days, and the middle one is about 194 days. At that period stacking reaches only about 10% (first star list) to 19% (second list) of our stars, even in the best case. The rule said skip, so we skipped.
2. **We looked at the false alarms that get through.** They are not white noise. Our nine checks stop about 98% of upside-down events, at every brightness; the 2% that get through look like short glitches. On both star lists the 3 hour box held most of them: 14 of 32 and 16 of 25.
3. **We read how a published search handles the same problem** (the mono-cbp paper, Davies and others, 2026). It reaches the same conclusion: most things that pass are leftover systematics. Its extra check, that a dip must survive many smoothing settings, costs up to two thirds of the shallow long dips, the same trade we had already measured.
4. **We tested a box floor.** The idea: drop the 3 hour box, the false alarms go, we gain room to loosen another check. We wrote the rule and the hope before running anything. Results on both lists: with the box gone, the false alarms did not go. They moved to the 4 hour box. The count of short-box survivors stayed at 17 on the first list and went from 20 to 16 on the second. The floor cost 7 to 8 points of recovery for 4 hour dips and bought nothing significant. Combined with the looser step check, false alarms stayed at 10 to 12 per 1,000 star-sectors against our limit of 8.
5. **We filed every result, including the failures,** in the plan, the version table and a shopping list for the next version. Nothing was tuned on the results, and nobody opened a real event.

## The idea behind it

**Idea (write the rule before the result):** the stacking gate and the box-floor test were written down first, so the answer could not bend to what we wished. The gate said skip, and we skipped; the test said no gain, and we recorded no gain.

**Idea (an exchange rate):** for every idea that did work, the gain in real dips was about equal to the gain in false alarms. When a filter sits at a balance like that, the right question is not "which trick?" but "how many false alarms are we willing to pay for how many planets?"

**Idea (where something is found is not what causes it):** the false alarms were most common in the 3 hour box, so I blamed the box. But when we removed it, the same glitches were found by the next box. A count by box shows where a thing is caught first. To find the cause you must ask: if I remove this, where does it go?

**Idea (check a borrowed rule's conditions):** I used a published "smoothing window is three times the transit" rule in a case where its conditions did not hold (our smoothing does not mask the dip). It cost a quarter of an 8 hour dip. Read when a rule applies, not just what it says.

## How you could do it yourself

- Look up the periods yourself on the [NASA Exoplanet Archive](https://exoplanetarchive.ipac.caltech.edu/) (filter for circumbinary, transiting) and take the median.
- Re-run the box-floor test: from the project folder run `cd pipeline`, then `..\.venv\Scripts\python.exe test_vopts.py` (a minute; it prints PASS lines). The full runs take hours at low priority.
- Read the three tables in `research\stage3\VESPER-VERSIONS.md` and look at the last column: it is the reason each idea was not accepted.

## What we found, and what we did not

- **Found (CONFIRMED, our own runs and the archive):** stacking reaches few stars at the periods most known planets have; the false alarms that get through are short glitches; dropping the 3 hour box moves them to the 4 hour box; every option tried trades about one real gain for one false alarm.
- **Found (LIKELY):** the filter is near a balance, so only new information (a physical duration limit per star, or repeat transits for the short-period planets) can improve it without a cost.
- **Not tested:** a physical duration limit per star (needs star radii, a download that needs your yes); a higher bar only for the shortest boxes; the repeated-transit check for the short-period end; any real event.
- **Uncertain:** how many of our binaries can show a 3 hour transit at all.

## Questions

??? quiz "1. Why did we write the stacking rule before reading the archive?"
    So the answer could not be shaped by what we hoped for. A rule written first is a test; a rule written after the result is a story.

??? quiz "2. The 3 hour box held most false alarms. Why did removing it not help?"
    The false alarms are short glitches, and the 4 hour box catches the same glitches. The box showed where they were found, not what made them.

??? quiz "3. What does 'one for one' mean here, and why does it matter?"
    For each extra real dip we recover we also let through about one extra false alarm. So there is no free improvement left in tuning; the number of false alarms we accept has to be decided on purpose.

??? quiz "4. Why is it good news that the idea failed?"
    We know why, we filed it so nobody repeats it, and the next step is clear. A clean, measured null is the win condition of this project.

## Later the same day: the v8 design

With the testing round finished, we wrote the next version. Three choices were open (how many false alarms to accept, whether to keep the shortest box, how to weigh the dip lengths). For each one I kept the rule we had written before the tests, because changing a rule after seeing which options fall just short of it is a quiet way of tuning on results. You can still change any of them before the freeze.

So v8 is the old filter plus one new check: **the orbit-locked flag.** A dip that repeats in another sector at exactly a whole number of the binary's own orbits, with the same length and depth, is most likely a leftover eclipse, not a planet, because a planet keeps its own clock. On the development stars it removes 49 events on 7 stars, the real-minus-control excess drops from +65 to +20, and it costs about 0.8% of planted fake planets. On the first star list it changes nothing. The check is built, tested and gives exactly the numbers we measured offline. It is not frozen yet: the freeze and the sealed stars wait for your go.

??? quiz "5. Why is a dip that repeats at a whole number of binary orbits suspicious?"
    The binary's eclipses keep the binary's clock. A planet orbits more slowly on its own clock, so its transits do not land on whole numbers of binary orbits except by chance.

Later still: you approved, and v8 is now **frozen** (Vesper 0.8.0, fingerprint 5f73692944aa). Before freezing we re-ran the old checks with the new flag on: 300 of 300 sample star-sectors give exactly the same events and tiers on the first star list, and all old tests pass. One old test failed first: its made-up events had no depth, which every real event has. The fix was to the test, not the filter.
