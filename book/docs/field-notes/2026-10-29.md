# 29 October 2026: how small a planet could we have found?

<p class="ss-meta">Field note · day 11 · about 6 min</p>

!!! plain "In plain words"
    We have searched 549 stars with Vesper and found nothing above noise. Today we asked the question that gives that result its meaning: if a planet had been there, how big would it have to be for us to catch it? We pasted thousands of fake dips into our own data and ran the whole frozen search on them. The answer is humbling. Only the biggest planets would have been caught, so "we found nothing" is a weak statement. It is still an honest one, and now it has numbers.

## What we did, and why

1. **We checked the fill.** Our "cleaned light" input fills some missing readings from the raw light. We counted: 0.67% of readings are filled, and a filled reading sits inside 8.5% of real dips and 7.8% of upside-down dips, so it affects both sides alike {{claim:F-086}}. No error was swallowed in secret. The run logs hold zero errors; the only silent skips are 21 QLP files with no safe time windows.
2. **We found a wrong name.** Of the 14 first-tier dips we called "cleaned light", 10 sit on QLP's raw flux, because QLP has no cleaned column before sector 56 {{claim:F-087}}. The comparison with the upside-down copy still holds, since both sides use the same flux, but the word "cleaned" was wrong. The briefing now says so, and the code gets the new name at the next freeze.
3. **We built a test that sends fake dips through the whole chain.** It pastes the same fake dip, at the same time and depth, into every file of a star-sector. Then the unchanged frozen code runs: search, checks, second look, raw-light twin, union and tiers. With nothing pasted in, it reproduces the real events in all 299 star-sectors tried, so it is the same machine.
4. **We ran 14,352 fake dips** in 299 random star-sectors, 4 lengths, 6 depths, each as a dip and as a mirror bump. Even a very deep dip (18 times the noise) is seen almost every time, but only 44% to 64% end in the first tier. Half are recovered at about 11 times the 8 hour noise, which is about 8,000 parts per million for the middle star {{claim:F-088}}.
5. **We compared that with the known planets.** We asked the NASA Exoplanet Archive for the sizes of known circumbinary planets and worked out the deepest dip each could make. Against 8,000 ppm only Kepler-16 b clears the bar for a middle star. Against the quietest bright stars (about 3,100 ppm) roughly half of the transiting ones do.
6. **We tested the mirror.** A fake dip and its mirror bump pass through the chain almost equally well: 32% against 34% {{claim:F-089}}. That supports using the upside-down copy as a control, within about two points.
7. **We counted the next star list (G2).** The catalogue we already had gives 895 new stars under the same rule as G1. I first guessed 1 to 4 GB of downloads. The query said about 28 GB, because every star has many sectors. I wrote my wrong guess down next to the right number.
8. **One slip of mine:** I pointed the browser at a PDF link and it offered to save the file on your PC. You cancelled it and nothing was saved. The new rule: I ask before touching any file link.

## The idea behind it

**Idea (injection and recovery):** to learn what a search could find, paste fake signals of known size into real data and count how many come out. The fraction is the completeness. It must be measured through the whole chain, because a dip that is found can still be thrown out by a check.

**Idea (a ceiling is information):** at 18 times the noise a dip is seen 99% of the time but kept only about 55% of the time. The missing 45% is the price of the checks. A check that protects against false alarms also loses real dips, and both sides of that trade should be measured.

**Idea (depth first, radius second):** a planet's dip depth is its radius over the crossed star's radius, squared, times the share of light that star gives. We know neither number for a binary, and the catalogue radius describes the blend. So we report depth and length first, and planet size only as a labelled range.

**Idea (a name can mislead):** "cleaned light" turned out to be mostly raw QLP flux. When a label hides what is inside, count the contents.

**Idea (a guess is not a measurement):** my 1 to 4 GB guess was wrong by a factor of seven. A cheap query replaced it. Check before you plan around a number.

**Idea (test the control, not only the data):** the upside-down copy is only a fair control if every check treats it like the data. The mirror test checks that for planet-like signals.

## How you could do it yourself

- Print the whole recovery table from the saved rows: from the project folder run `cd pipeline`, then `..\.venv\Scripts\python.exe chain_recovery.py summary v7`.
- Re-run the faithfulness test: `..\.venv\Scripts\python.exe chain_recovery_test.py` (a few minutes). It prints PASS lines.
- Look up a known planet's size yourself on the [NASA Exoplanet Archive](https://exoplanetarchive.ipac.caltech.edu/) and compute (planet radius over star radius) squared.

## What we found, and what we did not

- **Found (VERIFIED, our own runs):** the fill is 0.67% and acts on both sides alike; the whole chain recovers at most 44% to 64% of very deep dips; half are recovered at about 11 times the 8 hour noise; a dip and its mirror bump are recovered about equally (32% against 34%).
- **Found (VERIFIED by counting):** 10 of the 14 "cleaned-light" first-tier dips are on QLP raw flux.
- **Found (LIKELY):** a null on our 549 stars excludes giant planets and quiet bright stars, not typical circumbinary planets. The depths of the known ones are upper bounds, because their star's companion adds light.
- **Found (LIKELY):** the flat-baseline check costs long dips most (10% of short dips, 37% of 20 hour dips), probably because the 2 day smoothing window absorbs part of a long dip. NOT TESTED.
- **Not tested:** planet size from the real crossed-star radius and light share (not in our table); the 90% level (our depth grid stopped at 18 times the noise); false alarms per check set against dips lost per check; any real event. Nobody has looked at any event.
- **Uncertain:** whether the saved TESS binary catalogue's periods were redone with the later sectors; how much of the 28 GB is 20 second files.

## Questions

??? quiz "1. A fake dip is seen at SNR 7 in 99% of runs but ends in the first tier 55% of the time. Where did the other 44% go?"
    Into the checks. Flags for data gaps, ramps, a non-flat baseline, eclipses, shared moments and repeats move a found dip to tier 2. Quote both numbers: found, and kept.

??? quiz "2. Why do we report dip depth before planet radius?"
    Depth is what we measure. Radius needs the crossed star's radius and its share of the light, and for a binary the catalogue gives neither. The radius is a range with assumptions, not a measurement.

??? quiz "3. Kepler-16 b makes a dip of about 14,000 ppm and Kepler-38 b about 500 ppm. Which could our search catch at 50%?"
    Only the first, and only around a middle star (50% needs about 8,000 ppm). The second is far below even the best case of about 3,100 ppm.

??? quiz "4. What does the mirror-bump test check?"
    Whether the checks treat a dip in the real path and a bump in the upside-down path alike. If they do, the upside-down copy is a fair control for planet-like signals. Here they differ by about two points, which is small.

??? quiz "5. We guessed 1 to 4 GB and the query said 28 GB. What was the mistake?"
    I used the sector count in the table, but the archive holds every sector TESS has released since. When a number drives a plan, measure it with the cheapest check first.

??? quiz "6. Why was 'cleaned light' a misleading name?"
    10 of the 14 dips it covers sit on QLP's raw flux column, since QLP has no cleaned column before sector 56. The label hid what was inside. Count the contents before trusting the name.
