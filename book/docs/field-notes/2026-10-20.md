# 20 October 2026: testing honestly

<p class="ss-meta">Field note · night 1 and morning · about 12 min</p>

!!! plain "In plain words"
    Overnight we built a better planet search and then spent most of our effort trying to **break** it: hiding fake planets to see which it finds, flipping the data upside down to count false alarms, and running it on a real G1 star. In the morning, a bug in the professionals' tool (STANLEY) was found and worked around, and we measured our search with fake planets that move like real ones. **No new planet.** But we now know what our search can and can't see.

## What we did, and why

1. **Detector version 2.** It slides a box of 3 to 15 hours along the light curve and asks "is it darker in here than usual?" (Chapter 8). It re-found TOI-1338 b in 4 of 8 sectors {{claim:F-014}}.
2. **Fake planets.** We added made-up transits of known sizes and checked which were found: down to about 6 Earth radii {{claim:F-015}}.
3. **Upside-down data.** Flip the light curve (2 − brightness) and any dip becomes a bump. Whatever the search still "finds" must be noise: 6 fakes in 10 sectors {{claim:F-016}}.
4. **A failed clean-up.** Throwing out dips near spacecraft events removed fakes but also half the real crossings. So those became **warning flags** for a human (Chapter 10).
5. **A timing check.** A 2024 paper predicted a TOI-1338 b crossing on 16 December 2025. We found it {{claim:F-011}}, 56 ± 5 minutes after the predicted time.
6. **G1 frozen, QLP fixed, auto eclipse finder, Kepler-16 dry run.** The one dip on Kepler-16 was the telescope wobbling before a thruster firing. Nothing survived.
7. **STANLEY** installed and run (Chapter 12). Its strongest signal was near 96 days (first misread as 94; corrected in the afternoon), close to the real planet's 95.2, but its scores aren't calibrated yet.
8. **Compass** reviewed our upgrade plans: **REVISE**. Its three points: use STANLEY's fake-planet maker instead of building one; odd and even depths only mean something for planets that repeat; centroid checks must predict the expected shift.
9. **The STANLEY bug.** Its fake-planet maker placed **zero** transits. Cause: before adding the transits it ran them through its **search scorer**, which keeps only transits already showing a dip. Before injection there's no dip, so it kept none. Our wrapper now takes the transit times straight from its orbit simulation.
10. **The realistic test.** 6 fake planets, 33 crossings lasting 6.8 to 15.3 hours {{claim:F-029}}. Our cleaning kept 90 to 100% of each dip. 6 Earth radii: 8 of 10 found. 8: 10 of 10. 4: none. **3 of 33 crossings were lost inside the stars' eclipses** before the search even looked.

## The idea behind it

**Injection and recovery** is how you measure a search: add signals you know, see what comes back. It turns "we found nothing" into "nothing bigger than X crossed". Without it, a null result means nothing. Chapter 9 is all about it.

**Debugging by stepping through:** the STANLEY bug was found by calling its pieces one at a time. Did the orbit calculation give transit times? Yes, 13 to 29. Did they survive the next step? No. The fault sat between those two calls.

## What we found, and what we didn't

- **Found:** the search's floor (about 6 Earth radii), a 9% hidden loss inside eclipses, two STANLEY bugs, and a prediction confirmed.
- **Didn't:** any new planet. G1 hasn't been searched yet, apart from Kepler-16.
- **Mistakes, logged:** the first strict clean-up rule; a rule (C7) coded differently from how it was written; a cache that nearly wrote to C:; estimated timestamps written instead of checked ones.

## Questions

??? quiz "1. Why flip the light curve upside down?"
    A real dip becomes a bump, so the search can't find real planets in flipped data. Anything it finds there shows how often noise alone fools it.

??? quiz "2. 3 of 33 fake crossings fell inside eclipses. What does that mean for G1?"
    Even a perfect search misses about 1 crossing in 11, because we cut out the stars' eclipses before searching. Our "nothing found" results must count that loss.

## Afternoon: the Bible Overhaul begins

You paused the hunt so the book could catch up. Two lessons came out of the overhaul itself:

- **Labs are real runs.** Every command in Labs 0 to 4 was run on your PC before it went in the book, and the "what you should see" boxes are pasted output.
- **Writing a lab caught a mistake.** Our 19 October noise number for Kepler-16 (236 ppm per hour) came from a quick method that flattens away slow wobble. Measured the way our search sees the data, it's about 900 ppm; NASA's own file says 542 {{claim:F-039}}. Same data, a factor of 4 apart, depending on the method. [Chapter 7](../book/07-noise-and-statistics.md) teaches why. It's logged in the mistakes register, and it probably explains the early "2 to 5 Earth radii" overstatement.

??? quiz "3. Why did one star's one-hour noise come out anywhere from 229 to 908 ppm?"
    Each method flattens the light curve differently first. The harder you flatten, the more slow wobble you remove, and the smaller the noise looks. Quote the number that matches how your search treats the data.

**Later, batch 3 of the overhaul:** writing [Lab 8](../labs/lab8-read-stanley.md) caught a second slip. STANLEY's strongest orbit is at **96.1 days**, not the 93.8 reported overnight. The search log prints the period range high to low, but the results file runs low to high. The real planet's period is 95.2 days, so the conclusion ("close") survives, but the number was wrong for half a day. Lesson: read an array's order from the data file, and check one known feature lines up. [Chapter 12](../book/12-stanley.md) and [Chapter 6](../book/06-orbits-three-body.md) are new.
