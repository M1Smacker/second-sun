# 19 October 2026: how a research project starts

<p class="ss-meta">Field note · day 1 · about 8 min</p>

!!! plain "In plain words"
    We didn't touch a single light curve until we knew three things: what's already been found, where nobody has looked, and whether the data is good enough to look there. That took the whole day, and it decided our first campaign: **G1**, Kepler's two-star systems re-watched by TESS.

## What we did, and why

1. **Set the rules first.** 25 rounds of questions fixed what we hunt, how sure we must be before calling something a candidate, and which decisions are yours. Rules made before the data can't be bent to fit the data.
2. **Census of what's known.** Only **14** transiting two-star planets are known: 12 from Kepler and 2 from TESS {{claim:F-001}}. That's a tiny club, so even one new member would matter.
3. **Gap check.** Where hasn't anyone looked? Normal planet searches look for dips that repeat on a timetable. Two-star planets break the timetable (Chapter 5), so they're under-searched in TESS data.
4. **Measured the data before choosing.** I downloaded real files and measured their size {{claim:F-026}} and their noise. A quick estimate said one hour of Kepler-16's data wobbles by only 236 parts per million {{claim:F-027}}. *Corrected on 20 October:* that quick method was too optimistic; measured the way our search sees the data it's about 900 ppm {{claim:F-039}} (Chapter 7). The decision still stood, because the injection test measured what we can actually find.
5. **Chose G1.** Kepler found thousands of eclipsing binaries {{claim:F-030}}. Two-star planets' orbits slowly tilt ("precess"), so their transits switch on and off over years {{claim:F-009}}. A planet that was "off" during Kepler could be "on" now in TESS. You approved it from your phone.
6. **Founded this book,** with your rule: professional, clean, not gamey.

## The idea behind it

- **A research gap** is a question nobody has answered with the data that exists. Ours: has anyone searched TESS data of Kepler's binaries for single, irregular transits? The census and the reading said: not systematically.
- **Measure, don't guess.** "Is TESS good enough?" was answered with a number from a real file, not an opinion. You'll see this rule all through the book.

## How you could do it yourself

- **The census:** NASA's Exoplanet Archive lets anyone list known planets. We asked for every planet flagged as orbiting two stars.
- **The noise:** in [Lab 2](../labs/lab2-get-the-data.md) you download the same kind of file. Chapter 7 shows how to measure its noise.

## What we found, and what we didn't

- **Found:** a clear campaign (G1), a measured data budget, and a list of rivals to watch.
- **Not yet:** any searching. Day 1 was all preparation, on purpose.
- **One thing went wrong:** timed helper sessions froze on a permission prompt about 10 seconds after starting, so the research continued in the main session. Lesson: test automation before trusting it overnight.

## Questions

??? quiz "1. Why decide the rules before looking at any data?"
    So the data can't bend them. If you set the bar after seeing a signal, it's too easy to set it just below that signal.

??? quiz "2. Why might a planet missed by Kepler be visible to TESS?"
    Its orbit slowly tilts (precesses). Transits switch on and off over years, so it may have been "off" during Kepler (2009 to 2013) and "on" since.
