# 4 November 2026: the G3 search ran once and found nothing, and what that does and does not mean

<p class="ss-meta">Field note · day 17 · about 8 min</p>

!!! plain "In plain words"
    We ran the third and last test search, once, on 611 stars it had never seen. It took 8 minutes. It found no more real dips than random noise would give, so there is no excess and no lead. That is the clean null we aimed for. It is not proof that these stars have no planets: the stars are faint, so the search could only have seen large ones.

## What we did, and why

1. **Ran the search once.** The rules had been frozen and committed first (Vesper 0.8.1), so nothing could be tuned after seeing a result. The go came from Ahmed at about 01:09; the search ran from 01:15 to 01:20 on 8 workers.
2. **Scored it with the rules written before.** The test counts real dips against dips found in the flipped (upside-down) light, which is the noise control, and asks whether the real count is clearly higher than the noise predicts.
3. **Measured what it could have seen.** We planted fake dips of known depth into 300 random star-sectors and counted how many came out the other end (completeness).
4. **Looked at no individual star.** The page showed counts only while it ran, and the result was read from totals.

## What we found, and what we did not

- **Found (CONFIRMED): no excess.** At the main bar (signal-to-noise 7) there were 18 real dips against 31 upside-down ones, and noise alone predicts 23.5 real. Real was below the noise expectation at all four bars. The most the data allow is about 8 real dips above noise (95%).
- **Found (CONFIRMED): the false-alarm line misses its mark again.** 9.34 false alarms per 1,000 star-sectors against a mark of 8 or fewer (the earlier run gave 8.91). The interval, 6.39 to 13.19, includes the mark, so it is a miss by a small margin, twice in a row.
- **Found (CONFIRMED): completeness is modest.** A planted dip lasting 8 hours was found and kept 44% of the time overall, and 60% at the deepest setting. A Jupiter-size dip (10,000 ppm) is kept about 60% of the time; a dip like TOI-1338 b's, about 6%; a 4-Earth-radius one, about 3%.
- **Found (CONFIRMED): 14% of the light-curve reads could not be searched** (784 of 5,634), because no safe good-time windows were available for them.
- **Not tested:** a real planet. None of these stars is a known circumbinary host, so there was nothing to recover.
- **Uncertain:** the exact planet-rate limits. They assume a 1 solar-radius star and no dilution; the real star sizes are not in yet.

## Three lessons

1. **"Fewer real dips than noise" is still "consistent with zero".** The real count came out 5 below the noise expectation, but the 95% range runs from 19 below to 11 above. We read it as no excess, not as a deficit.
2. **A null is only as strong as what the search could see.** The same result means different things for a Jupiter and for a small planet. The completeness table is part of the answer, not a footnote.
3. **Check the machine a minute after launch.** The page feed did not start at first, because a text label with spaces was split into pieces. We found it within minutes because we looked. The fix is a rule: after every launch, check the feed is alive.

## Questions

1. Why is "no excess" different from "no planets"?
2. The real count was lower than the noise expectation. Why do we not say real dips are rarer than noise?
3. Why do we plant fake dips into the data?

Answers: 1. No excess says the real count was not higher than noise. It says nothing about planets the search could not see, such as small ones around faint stars. 2. The difference is smaller than the scatter, so it is consistent with zero. 3. It is the only way to measure how often a real dip of a known size would survive every step.

## Night addition: where the next list comes from

Written the same night, after the Census plan. Nothing was searched; we only counted and checked our own numbers.

- **Found (CONFIRMED): there is a lot of unlisted fuel.** TESS watched 476,784 different stars at two-minute cadence in sectors 27 to 109. Of these, 364,656 (258,858 at magnitude 13.5 or brighter) were never two-minute targets in sectors 1 to 26, the sectors the published eclipsing-binary list covers. No list names the eclipsing binaries among them.
- **Found (LIKELY, rough):** at the share the published list found (1.97% of stars), those bright new stars would hold about 5,100 eclipsing binaries, and about 1,000 of the type we searched in G2 (long period, detached, bright). The error is at least a factor of 2.
- **Found (CONFIRMED): the reads we could not search are a supply problem.** The 784 unsearched reads are 16% of the star-sectors. The share is 4% in sectors 1 to 26, 27% in sectors 27 to 55 and 18% after, because our safe-window borrowing needs nearby two-minute stars and we only have them where a list exists. Searching them would have added only about 8% to the reach.
- **Found (CONFIRMED and UNCERTAIN): our reach map is too generous.** If the Kepler rate of 10% held, our three searches should have found 13 to 22 planets by the map as it stands, and they found none. That cannot be taken at face value: the map counts the chance that a transit falls in the observed days but not the chance that the planet transits at all. A rough cross-check against Kepler's own numbers says the map is too high by several times. How much is NOT tested.
- **Found (LIKELY): the size that matters.** If the geometry factor is about a quarter, about 1,000 bright two-minute stars is where a null first tests the 10% rate, and 2,000 to 3,000 would test its low end.
- **Found (LIKELY): the missing factor is about 0.05 to 0.2.** A later probe took the eclipse width of every star and asked whether a planet in the binary's own plane would cross the star at all. For most binaries it would not, so the chance falls to roughly 5% to 20% of what the map assumed. With that, our three searches expected about 1 to 4 planets at Kepler's rate (up to 7 in the most optimistic corner: a small companion star and a few degrees of tilt) and had between 0% and 50% chance of finding none. In most of the cases we tried the null is consistent with Kepler's rate and could not exclude it; in the most optimistic corner it is not, so the question stays open. The size of the companion star is the biggest unknown.
- **Found (CONFIRMED): fetching the light is cheaper than G3 made it look.** A public Amazon bucket gave 3 files a second with 8 threads, 1.8 MB a file. G3's download ran for 21 hours of wall clock, but about 20 of them were a stall: it hit a server time-out after 410 of 612 stars, sat idle until it was restarted the next night, and the restart finished the last 202 stars in about 10 minutes. The real work was about an hour and a quarter. All 5,193 new bright stars with 8 or more sectors would take about 7 hours and 135 GB.
- **Not tested:** the geometry condition on the known Kepler planets, and a larger fetch run. Both come before any new star list is sized.

### Three more lessons

4. **A limit needs every factor in it.** A map of what a search could see is only an upper bound until it includes the chance that a planet transits at all. Quote it as an upper bound until then.
5. **When the data feeding a rule come from a list, the rule fails where the list ends.** The borrowed windows came from our own stars, which come from catalogues that stop at sector 26. The gap shows up exactly after that sector.
6. **A slow download can be a stall, not the size.** G3 took 21 hours of wall clock but was working for about an hour and a quarter; the rest was a time-out that nobody noticed. The same kind of file from a public bucket moves at 5 MB a second. Measure the route on a few files, and make a download shout when it stops, before you plan around the hours.

### More questions

4. Why can we not yet quote the reach numbers as limits on planets?
5. Why were more reads lost after sector 26 than before it?
6. Why did G3 take 21 hours to download 2.2 GB when the real work took about an hour?

Answers: 4. The map leaves out the chance that a planet transits at all, so it is too high; a probe puts the missing factor at roughly 0.05 to 0.2, not yet checked on known planets. 5. The safe good-time windows are borrowed from nearby two-minute stars, and our two-minute stars come from lists that stop at sector 26. 6. A stall, not the size: the download hit a time-out, sat idle for about 20 hours before it was restarted, and the restart finished in 10 minutes; a public bucket moves the same kind of file at a similar speed.

### Morning addition: does the shape of a dip matter?

- **Found (CONFIRMED): yes, a little for real planets and a lot for odd shapes.** Our planted dips were flat-bottomed with short slopes. We replanted the same dips in other shapes through the same untouched search. A dip with long rounded slopes was kept 21.5% of the time against 30.5% for our flat-bottomed one. A dip shaped like a real planet crossing a star (the star is darker at its edge, the planet is a round disc) was kept 27.1% to 27.8% of the time, about 3 points or a tenth less.
- **Found (LIKELY): our quoted completeness is about a tenth too high.** The search matches a box shape, and a rounder dip fits a box worse. The loss is biggest for shallow and short dips, and it disappears for loud ones.
- **Not tested:** other planet sizes, grazing crossings, and the way a circumbinary planet's speed changes during a crossing.

7. **A planted signal has a shape, and the shape is a choice.** Check the answer for two or three shapes before you trust a completeness number; the realistic one moved ours by about a tenth.

7. Why did the completeness number fall when we planted rounder dips? Answer: the search slides a box along the light; a rounder dip fits the box less well, so more of the shallow ones slip below the line.
