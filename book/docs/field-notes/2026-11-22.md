# 22 November 2026: back to work, and a smart-looking idea that did not help

<p class="ss-meta">Field note · day 35 · about 4 min</p>

!!! plain "In plain words"
    Work restarted tonight with a bigger budget. Two tests run on the machine while a third gave its answer: letting a computer learn one combined score for the held-back test planets does not help. It rescues almost nothing for real dips and helps the upside-down copy more, which is unfair. So the effort moves to catching fainter dips in the first place.

## Where the missing planets go

Out of every 100 planted test planets that can exist, today's filter finds about 48. The rest are lost in three places:

| Where | Points |
|---|---|
| found | 48.4 |
| too faint: every crossing under the bar | 23.4 |
| seen, then held back by a warning flag | 16.7 |
| never scored (masked, no safe data) | 11.6 |

## Tonight's test: one learned score

**Learned score** (new word): instead of four separate warning flags that each hold an event on their own, a simple model weighs everything at once (strength, depth, length, how flat the sides are, whether a second copy of the light agrees) and gives one number. The model learns its weights from planted planets (should pass) and from events in untouched light (should not).

The rules were written down before the test ran. It had to rescue at least 2 more planets in 100, keep false alarms on both the real and the upside-down copy no higher than today, and treat both copies fairly.

| | Real dips | Upside-down copy |
|---|---|---|
| change in planets found | +0.5 | +3.4 |

It failed. It rescued as many planets as it newly held back, and it helped the upside-down copy far more than the real one. The reason is one we have met before: next to data gaps and spacecraft thruster firings, fake dips are much more common than fake bumps. A rule that relaxes there pays in real false alarms the upside-down copy cannot see. A quick side check on another flag (the second-copy check) found it is worth at most 0.3 points.

## What runs now

1. **D1 on the helper machine.** The notch (each dip judged against the quieter of its two sides) for dips of 8 hours or less, the old score for longer ones. It is judged on brand-new test planets, because the old ones were already seen.
2. **A window test on our side.** The standard advice in the field is to smooth the light with a window about 3 times the dip's length. We compare that, alone and with the notch, at equal false alarms.

## Check yourself

??? question "Why did the learned score help the upside-down copy more than the real one?"
    Because the untouched real light is full of fake dips near gaps and thruster firings that look like planted dips, while the upside-down copy has no such fake bumps. The score learned to distrust dips, which costs real planets more.

??? question "Why judge D1 on new test planets?"
    The old test planets were already looked at when D1 was designed. A fair judge must be something the design never saw.
