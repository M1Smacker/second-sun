# 14 November 2026: the spacecraft's own hiccups, and a blind spot in our fairness check

<p class="ss-meta">Field note · day 27 · about 6 min</p>

!!! plain "In plain words"
    Every few days TESS fires its thrusters to slow its spinning wheels (a momentum dump). For about a day before that, its pointing wobbles more and more, so a little of each star's light spills outside the box we measure it in. That makes a small dip that looks like a planet. Our upside-down check cannot see these fake dips, because a wobble only ever loses light: it never makes a bump.

## What we found

1. **A known-events flag, adopted.** NASA's sector notes list 19 moments when the telescope misbehaved (pointing shifts, tests, heater changes). Holding events that touch them costs 2 of about 1,100 test planets and removes 4 upside-down and 2 real false alarms. Codex checked it with its own code and got the same numbers. Ahmed adopted it for the next filter version.
2. **A rule that passes every test and is still not safe.** Judging dips near dumps by their shape (is the light around them even?) lifts our planet score to 49.98%, with no extra upside-down alarms. But it lets back in 30 real events against only 4 upside-down ones, and 23 of the 30 sit just before a dump. The papers say why: the wobble grows before each dump (Vanderburg 2019) and lingers for hours after it (Eisner 2019).
3. **The lesson.** A pass rule is only as good as its meter. Our false-alarm meter is the upside-down copy of the light, and it is blind to anything that only makes dips. So the rule waits for Fable on Monday, and Codex is measuring exactly where around a dump the fake dips appear.
4. **Equal eclipses fool a period search.** Codex tried to time every binary's eclipses from TESS itself. It failed on stars whose two eclipses look alike: it saw an eclipse every half orbit and called that the period (13 of 58 control stars). On the right period the timing was good to about a minute and a half. The fix is to compare alternate eclipses before halving.

New word: **aperture loss**, light from the star falling outside the pixels we add up, so the star looks fainter than it is.

## Check yourself

1. Why can the upside-down copy not catch a dump artefact? (A wobble only removes light from the aperture, so it makes dips and never bumps.)
2. Why is "passes every test" not enough? (The tests share one meter; if the meter is blind to an effect, every test is too.)
3. Why does a binary with two equal eclipses look like it has half its period? (Each half orbit shows an identical dip, so folding at half the period lines them all up.)
