## The null so far

"No excess" means the real light did not show more planet-like dips than its upside-down copy, which shows what noise alone makes. So: **found nothing**, three times, cleanly.

## How much the silence means

A null is only as strong as the search's eyes. In G3, a dip as deep as the known planet TOI-1338 b looks in TESS (about 0.23%) was caught only about 6 times in 100. A Jupiter-sized dip (1%) was caught about 60 times in 100. Small planets: no limit at all.

If circumbinary planets are as common as Kepler found, our three hunts together should have held roughly 1 to 3 planets we could see (an estimate, not a measurement). Finding none is therefore not surprising. The honest reading is **"we have not yet looked hard enough to tell"**, not "there are no planets here". That reading is the first key's only. No second key has checked it, and nothing is claimed outside this book.

## Later the same morning: the tool is not at its limit

Ahmed asked one hard question: was "the tool is reaching its limit" really true? A two-hour research stint said no, and Second Sun restarted at 11:23 am with one focus: raise P, the share of test planets we recover (today 48 in 100), toward 75.

!!! plain "In plain words"
    Most planets we miss are not too small for TESS. They hide under slow wobbles in the light that our cleaning step leaves behind. A new way of judging each dip, against the quieter of the two stretches of light beside it, catches about 7 more test dips in 100 without any extra false alarms.

What we measured, on 160 test stars with planted planets:

1. **The ceiling.** A perfect detector on today's data would see about 84 of 100 test planets. So 75 is possible, but only if almost nothing else is lost.
2. **Where the noise is.** For faint dips the search's noise is about three times what pure random noise would be. It is not star pulsations (removing them made things worse) and it is not mainly at data gaps.
3. **The new score.** A "notch" judges a dip against its own sides. Our first version was unfair: a bump beside the dip lifted the baseline, so the upside-down copy suddenly showed two and a half times more alarms. Using the lower of the two sides fixed it. At equal false alarms it found 74 of 100 planted dips instead of 66, and it found planted bumps on the upside-down copy just as often as dips (the fairness check).
4. **Next.** Codex now runs the new score through all of our filter rules. Later we add a second false-alarm meter: hundreds of ordinary bystander stars on the same camera chip, because when many unrelated stars dip at the same moment, the telescope made the dip.

New word: **notch**, a dip score measured against the light just before and after the dip, instead of against the whole sector.

## Check yourself (second part)

2. Why do we need bystander stars? (The telescope's own wobble only ever makes dips, so the upside-down copy cannot see it; many stars dipping at once can.)

## Afternoon: the notch meets every rule, and fails one

Codex and Claude each counted the full test, separately, with different code. They agree on every number.

!!! plain "In plain words"
    The notch helps short dips and hurts long ones. Our rules say a new score must not lose on the standard test, so it is not adopted. The idea is still good; it needs to know when to step aside.

1. **New test planets it never saw:** 54.3 of 100 found, against 52.2 before. The bar was +2.0, so it passes, only just.
2. **False alarms:** fewer, on both the real and the upside-down copy. It treats dips and bumps more evenly than the old score.
3. **The known planet TOI-1338 b:** 7 of its 13 crossings reach the top list, against 6 before.
4. **The standard test (T156):** 31.7 of 100, against 34.2 before. This one fails. Dips of 3 to 9 hours gain; dips of 9 hours or longer lose.

Why long dips lose (likely, not yet proven): the notch compares a dip with the light on both sides of it. For a long dip that side stretch has to be long too, and slow wobbles of the star creep in.
