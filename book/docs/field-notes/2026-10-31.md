# 31 October 2026: the search did see the known planet; our test used an old calendar

<p class="ss-meta">Field note · day 13 · about 6 min</p>

!!! plain "In plain words"
    After the sealed search gave its clean null, we spent a day asking why it came out that way. The biggest answer: the one known planet in the stars, TOI-1338 b, was not missed as badly as the score said. We had checked the wrong dates. With the dates published later, the search saw it in 7 of 10 passes in front of its star.

## What we did, and why

1. **We checked the known-planet test against newer papers.** Our test used the transit dates predicted in 2020. A 2024 paper shows those dates drift by up to a day or more, because a second planet pulls on the first. We did not change the old score (it was written down before the run). We added a second line, marked as written afterwards.
2. **We measured what the surviving false alarms look like.** Many upside-down false alarms start abruptly and recover slowly. That is the shape of a stellar flare turned upside down. Real planet transits are symmetric.
3. **We priced every safety check.** For each check we asked: if we switch only this one off, how many fake planets come back, and how many true ones do we gain?
4. **We counted in stars, not events.** A few stars carry many events, so counting events overstates how sure we are.
5. **We read how Kepler and ExoFOP handle the same problems** (links in the findings file).

## What we found, and what we did not

- **TOI-1338 b:** 7 of 10 passes have a real dip within about 1.7 hours of the dates in the 2024 paper; 4 of the 10 were kept as strong. Four were predicted by that paper before the data existed, and all four matched. (Chance of that happening by luck: well under one in a million.)
- **Flares:** about half of the false alarms that got through show the flare shape, against about one in eight of the real ones. A shape check could remove many of them at a small cost. We have not tested that cost yet.
- **Checks:** the step check buys the most planets back but costs about 7 fake ones per point; three smaller checks cost about 4 times less per point. We have not tested them together.
- **What the null rules out:** much less than "no planets". A single sector catches only about a quarter of 95 day transits.
- **How to report a find:** the official ExoFOP site now accepts only candidates that are already published, so a find goes through a paper or a collaborator first.
- **Not found:** how many human eyes are needed to beat the search, and whether the flare shape is really flares (no catalogue was compared).

## Questions

1. Why did we keep the old score and add a second line instead of changing it?
2. Why is a flare a problem for the upside-down control?
3. Why does counting stars give a more honest answer than counting events?

Answers: 1. The old score was fixed before the run; changing it afterwards would be tuning on results. 2. Turned upside down it becomes a short deep dip, which our edge checks do not catch. 3. Events from one star are not independent, so the same star counted many times looks like more evidence than it is.
