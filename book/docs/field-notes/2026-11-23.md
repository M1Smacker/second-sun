# 23 November 2026: when the reference table is the thing that is wrong

<p class="ss-meta">Field note · day 36 · about 4 min</p>

!!! plain "In plain words"
    Two checks this morning ended the same way: the method looked fine, and the table we were checking it against was wrong. Gaia's big list of eclipsing binaries tells us which stars are pairs, but its periods for slow pairs are mostly wrong. And our own new eclipse timer failed its test by one star, because for six stars the catalogue's eclipse times do not match the eclipses we can see in the light.

## Gaia's list: who, not when

Census, our future hunt on many more stars, needs a list of eclipsing binaries with the times of their eclipses, so the search can hide the eclipses first. Gaia's next release (2 December) brings 2.4 million eclipsing binaries. We tested the current release on stars whose eclipses TESS has already timed.

| Check | Result |
|---|---|
| Gaia period equals the TESS period, TESS Ten Thousand known binaries over 5 days (the paper's own column) | 8 of 306 (2.6%) |
| same, bright stars of the Prsa 2022 TESS list | 50 of 90 (56%) |
| period right, and the eclipse lands within half an eclipse width | 63% |

**Ephemeris** (new word): the two numbers that predict every eclipse of a binary, a period and one eclipse time. With them, the next eclipse is "that time plus a whole number of periods".

So Census takes the list of stars from Gaia and the ephemerides from our own measurement of TESS light.

## Our eclipse timer: failed by one, for an interesting reason

The timer measures every eclipse in TESS light and fits an ephemeris. Its test, written before it ran, needed 48 of 53 fresh test stars to agree with the catalogue. It got 47.

We then looked at the 6 that disagreed. For every one, the eclipses we see in the light sit 0.3 to 35 eclipse widths away from where the catalogue says, while our fit follows the visible eclipses to within 0.04 of a width. The catalogue was wrong for those stars, not the timer. The test stays failed in the record, because a result is never re-read after the fact. A new test, also written before it runs, now judges the timer by something it cannot fake: fit all sectors but the last, then predict where the eclipses fall in the last one.

## Check yourself

??? question "Why does a wrong catalogue make a good timer fail its test?"
    The test asked whether our timer agrees with the catalogue. When the catalogue itself is off, a correct timer disagrees with it. That is why the new test compares predictions with eclipses measured in the light.

??? question "Why is the failed test not simply passed after we found the reason?"
    Because a rule changed after seeing the result can be bent to fit any result. The fair move is a new test, written first, on stars the timer has never been judged on.
