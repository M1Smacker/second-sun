## The idea behind it

A map is a promise about where things are. To draw part of the sky on a flat page you need each star's **position** (right ascension and declination, like longitude and latitude on the sky) and a rule for flattening a sphere. We used a **gnomonic projection**, which keeps straight lines straight and distorts only a little over a patch this size. Brightness becomes dot size: a smaller **magnitude** number means a brighter star, so it gets a bigger dot. Colour comes from the star's spectral type. That is why the map is real: change the catalogue and the picture changes.

### Night: an outside brief on how to show you the dots, and what it taught us about our own practice

Another Claude, with web access, wrote a research brief on how a page should present a candidate dip to a beginner and to an AI without one anchoring the other. I did not build from it. I checked it first (our Compass review), and I read the original papers for its key claims.

- **What held up:** hiding the star's name until after the answer, hidden fake dips with known answers, an equal-sized "unclear" button, no planet-shaped model drawn on the data before both answers are in, and scoring "can you see dips" separately from "do you say planet too easily". The medical result that a wrong AI hint can drag even experts down to about 45% accuracy is reported consistently, but I only read news coverage of it, so I call it likely, not proven.
- **What would have been a mistake: the lineup test.** The idea is to hide the real dip among a few empty stretches and see if you can pick it out. I tried it in a simulation of pure noise with no planet at all. If the empty stretches are random, a perfect observer still picks "the dip" 84% of the time, because the dip was chosen as the biggest wiggle of the whole sector and a random stretch is not. If the empty stretches are the biggest wiggle found by the same search on other noise, the observer drops to 20%, which is pure chance, as it should be. The comparison is only fair when both sides were picked the same way.
- **What it showed about our own practice:** your "quiet" practice curves are random stretches. On real data you will only ever see the machine's best-looking wiggle in each sector. So your false-alarm score on easy quiet curves understates how often you would be fooled for real. The fix is a new kind of practice card, "noise at its best", built from the detector's top event on upside-down or noise-only data.
- **Our pass line, measured:** I simulated readers of known skill. Someone who truly finds 75% of dips and false-alarms 12% of the time meets the line in the first two rounds about 9% of the time; someone at 90% and 8% meets it only 55% of the time. So the line is a fair gate but a noisy one, and that is why I judge the numbers and do not just read the line.
- **What it changes:** the current Eyes practice teaches shapes, but it is a clean 36 hour cutout of one star. The real page will show a whole sector, a zoom, where the dip falls in the stars' own eclipse cycle and a second data product, on many stars with different noise. A new layout needs its own readiness test, so a rehearsal with hidden fakes in the real layout is now required before any real lead, not optional.
- **Nothing was built.** This was a review. The full verdict is in `research/compass-reviews/2026-10-24-vetting-page-research.md`.

**Idea:** a test is only fair if the thing you check and the thing you compare it with were chosen the same way. Picking the best-looking stretch of the data and comparing it with an ordinary stretch proves nothing.

**Idea:** before you ask whether you are good at spotting something, ask whether your practice material is as hard as the real material. Easy practice gives a flattering score.

- **How hard it really is:** for a perfect box-shaped filter, the hard-noise cards have a median strength of 6.1 and 30% reach 7 or above. The clear fakes beat every noise card. The near-limit fakes at 5 to 6.5 cannot be told from ordinary hard noise, so there *Not sure* is the right answer, not a dodge.
- **The test caught my first version.** I made the noise from the star's own leftovers, and the checks said the "noise" had a median strength of 19. The star's data holds real glitches, and reshuffling them put a huge dip in every card. The fix was to leave out any piece that already holds an obvious dip or bump. Without a check that measured the cards, I would have shipped a test full of hidden eclipses-by-accident.
- **Tested:** 15 checks of the cards and scoring, a browser run of a whole hard test and a hard practice round, and I broke the code on purpose 10 ways (unselected noise, an event always in the middle, weaker fakes, a missing gap, wrong *Not sure* scoring, the two lines mixing, a moved line); every break was caught.
- **Looking at the live page caught a second flaw.** In my first version, any card with a gap in the data was the "dip at a gap" card, so a gap alone gave the answer away. Now about 4 in 10 of the other cards carry a gap too, kept well away from the event, so only a dip that touches a gap counts. Two more deliberate breaks of that rule were caught.
- **Still one star.** All of this uses TOI-1338's sector 98. The rehearsal in step three uses many stars.

**Idea:** build a thing that makes fake data, then measure the fake data. If you never check that your fake noise behaves like noise, your whole test rests on a guess.

**Idea:** fix the pass mark before anyone plays. A line chosen after the scores are in is not a line.

## Questions

??? quiz "1. Why do bigger dots mean brighter stars on the map?"
    Magnitude is backwards: a smaller number is brighter. The map turns that into a dot size, so the brightest stars (like Deneb) are drawn largest.

??? quiz "2. Why does the caption say 546 drawn, not 549?"
    Three of our stars have no sky position in the Villanova Kepler catalogue, and we only draw what we can place. The book says so rather than rounding up.

??? quiz "3. Why did a dip that was obvious at 10-minute spacing vanish at 30-minute spacing?"
    The detector only trusted boxes that were at least 80% full of 10-minute bins. At 30-minute spacing two bins in three are empty, so no box ever qualified and the search returned nothing, with no error.

??? quiz "4. Seven stars said the noise was 1.76 times ideal; all 549 said about 2.5. Why trust the second number?"
    The first seven were brighter than most and had the best data. The 549 are the whole list, including faint stars where noise is worse. A sample only describes the population it was drawn from.

??? quiz "5. Why do we flag events near data gaps instead of deleting them?"
    Because a real planet can sit there. Two of the four TOI-1338 b crossings we re-found were within half a day of a gap or a thruster firing. Flagged events go to a lower-priority list for a person to check later.

??? quiz "6. The eclipse-phase check removes 17% of real events and 19% of fake ones. What kind of check is that?"
    A safety net, not a filter. It does not separate real from fake (the shares are about equal); it just catches a specific mistake, an eclipse the mask missed, at the price of ignoring the stretches near eclipses.

??? quiz "7. Why can the look-back button never change an answer?"
    The saving code only accepts the next unanswered question of a round that is still open. An old answer is never written again, and the tests try to change one and check that it fails.
