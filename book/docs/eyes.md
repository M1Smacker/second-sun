# Eyes mode

<p class="ss-meta">Hunt · practice · 5 to 100 light curves</p>

!!! quote "The story"
    In the first six quarters of Kepler data, volunteers on a website called Planet Hunters found a planet by looking at light curves by eye. It crosses the larger of two stars, which circle each other every 20 days, about once every 137 days, and it was named PH1 b {{claim:F-059}}. A second pair of stars, about 1,000 times the Earth-Sun distance away, makes it the first transiting planet known in a four-star system {{claim:F-059}}. Ahmed, that is why this book has an eyes mode: some planets have been found by people looking.

!!! plain "In plain words"
    You get a short stretch of real light curve with nothing marked on it. Say Dip, No dip, Odd or Not sure, and then the answer is shown. It is practice: the dips are fake planets we hid, plus glitches and real eclipses that we know about.

## How it works

- **Blind first.** Nothing is marked until you answer, then the hidden thing is outlined.
- **Four answers.** *Dip* is a planet-like dip that comes back up. *No dip* means nothing there. *Odd* is something that is not a planet: an eclipse, a spike or a sudden jump. *Not sure* is always allowed, and it is honest.
- **Keys.** On a keyboard, press 1 to 4 to answer and Enter for the next one.
- **You choose the length** of each sitting: 5, 20, 50 or 100 light curves.
- **Practice only.** Every stretch comes from the real TOI-1338 light curve (TESS sector 98) with answers we know. The real stars of the hunt are not in here: they wait until you say start.
- **Looking exercise.** This page has no text version, because the skill is seeing the picture. The [light-curve explorer](book/02-light-curves.md) in Chapter 2 and the numbers in Chapter 7 cover the same ideas in words.
