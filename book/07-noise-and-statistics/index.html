      7. Noise and statistics: when is a dip real?¶

      Chapter 7 · about 20 min · Maths: averages, square roots, one probability

      Uses the voice on your own device.

      1-minute summary

      • What: noise, signal to noise (SNR), and how chance alone makes fake dips.
      • Why: a dip only counts if it is clearly bigger than the wobble over the same length of time.
      • You will be able to: say how sure a dip is, and why averaging does not fix slow drifts.

      The story

      To find out how often noise alone fools us, we turned TOI-1338's light curves upside down, so that no planet could be in them. Our detector still announced 6 dips in 10 sectors, one as strong as SNR 12.6 VERIFIED. Nothing was there. Noise is patient, and if you search enough places it will build you a planet. This chapter is how to tell the difference.

      In plain words

      Every measurement wobbles, and a dip only counts if it is clearly bigger than the wobble. Look in thousands of places and a few will fake a dip by pure chance.

      What this chapter answers: how big is the noise, why doesn't averaging always help, and how do we set a bar that noise rarely crosses?

      7.1 The intuition¶

      You weigh yourself on a cheap bathroom scale. It says 71.3, then 70.8, then 71.1. Each reading wobbles, but the average of 30 readings is much steadier than any one. That's random noise, and averaging beats it.

      Now imagine the floor slowly tilts during the morning. All 30 readings drift together. Averaging them doesn't remove the tilt: you get a steady, wrong number. That's correlated noise, called red noise, and averaging can't beat it.

      A planet's dip lasts hours. So the question is never "how much does one reading wobble?" It's "how much does a few-hour average wobble?" Slow drifts decide that.

      With JavaScript on, this spot shows a made-up light curve with a dip hidden in noise, and sliders to change the dip, the noise and the averaging.

      Try it. The grey dots are measurements and the amber line is their average. Turn the noise up and the dip sinks out of sight. Average more points together and it comes back, because random wobble shrinks when you average. Now turn on the slow drift: averaging stops helping, because a drift is not random wobble. This is why our detector measures the wobble over the same length of time as the dip, and why it first removes slow drifts with a 2-day filter. The numbers here are made up for the demonstration, and this is a harsh case.

      7.2 The scientific meaning¶

      Where the wobble comes from¶

      • Photon noise. Light arrives as separate particles (photons). Counting them is like counting raindrops in a bucket: the count naturally varies by about its square root. Brighter stars give more photons, so relatively less noise.
      • The telescope. Pointing jitter, heating, thruster firings, scattered light from Earth and Moon (Lab 2's quality flags).
      • The star itself. Stars have spots, flares and boiling surfaces (granulation) that change their brightness slowly.

      White and red noise¶

      White noise: each measurement's wobble is independent of the last one. Averaging N of them shrinks the wobble by √N.

      Red noise: neighbouring measurements wobble together. Averaging helps much less. Real TESS data always has some, and red noise makes transit searches much harder than white-noise maths suggests LIKELY.

      Signal-to-noise ratio¶

      The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) compares the dip's depth with the wobble of an average over the dip's length. SNR 7 means the dip is 7 times bigger than a typical few-hour wobble. NASA's planet search uses a bar of 7.1 LIKELY; ours is 7.

      The look-elsewhere effect¶

      If you check one spot, a 7-times wobble is incredibly rare. But a search checks every time and every dip length: thousands of tries per sector. Rare things happen when you try often enough. That's why our search also runs on upside-down data (Chapter 8): it counts how many fakes noise produces with the same number of tries.

      7.3 The technical explanation¶

      Measuring the wobble robustly¶

      The standard deviation (σ) is the usual measure of spread, but one wild point can inflate it. We use a robust version that ignores a few outliers:

      σ ≈ 1.4826 × median( | x − median(x) | )

      • Take each value's distance from the middle value, then the middle of those distances (the MAD, median absolute deviation).
      • The factor 1.4826 makes it equal σ for well-behaved (bell-curve) noise.

      Averaging¶

      σN = σ1 / √N

      • σ1: the wobble of one measurement. σN: the wobble of an average of N measurements.
      • Example: 2,015 ppm per 2-minute point, averaged over 1 hour (N = 30): 2,015 / √30 ≈ 368 ppm, if the noise were white.

      SNR¶

      SNR = depth / σD

      • σD: the wobble of an average over the dip's duration D, measured, not computed with Eq. 7.2.
      • Our detector measures σD directly: it takes every possible D-hour average in the sector and measures their robust spread. Red noise is automatically included.

      How rare is 7σ?¶

      For pure bell-curve noise, the chance of one average falling 7σ below normal is about 1 in 780 billion. A sector gives our search tens of thousands of tries (thousands of ten-minute positions × 8 box lengths). Pure white noise would almost never cross the bar. Yet on upside-down TOI-1338 data, noise crossed it 6 times in 10 sectors VERIFIED. That's the strongest evidence that real TESS noise isn't white or bell-shaped: it has slow drifts and sudden jumps.

      Noise is lopsided: the upside-down test and its limits¶

      A planet makes a dip, so an upside-down light curve has no planets in it, only noise. Every "dip" we find there is a false alarm, so the number of upside-down dips tells us how many fakes noise makes with the same search. That is a control: the same test, run where the right answer is known to be "nothing".

      The control has a flaw: noise is lopsided. Stars throw flares, which are sudden brightenings, and telescope glitches jump up more often than down. Turned upside-down, those become dips. So the upside-down curve makes more false dips than the real curve would make from noise alone. We measured it on 900 random star-months with no filters at all: at signal-to-noise 4.5 the upside-down curve has 1.25 times as many dips as the real one, and at 7 it has 2.3 times as many VERIFIED. Real planets would add dips to the real curve and push that ratio down. It went up, so there is no sign of planets, and the control is harsher than a fair one.

      One control is one opinion. NASA's Kepler team used two, inverted and scrambled light curves, and for long periods and faint signals they gave a reliability of 24% and 51% VERIFIED. Scrambling moves big blocks of time around but keeps the order inside each block VERIFIED, so a single dip survives it. That makes it the wrong control for a search for single dips like ours.

      Heavy tails. Bell-curve noise almost never reaches 7 sigma, yet real data does it again and again, because real noise has heavy tails: more big wobbles than the bell curve allows. Our signal-to-noise already divides by the measured spread of the box averages, so correlated (red) noise is included, but that spread is measured in the middle of the distribution and the far tail is fatter. NASA's Kepler search sets a false-alarm level for each star with a bootstrap VERIFIED. We do not do that yet.

      7.4 Our implementation¶

      In pipeline/detect_v2.py:

      Function What it does Why
      detrend() flattens slow trends with a 2-day "biweight" filter (from the wotan toolkit) removes star spots and drifts, but keeps dips up to 15 hours (Chapter 9 measured 90 to 100% kept)
      bin10() averages into 10-minute bins fewer, steadier points
      box_scores() for each length D, measures the robust spread of all D-hour averages: σD red-noise-aware SNR
      find_events() keeps boxes with SNR ≥ 7 the bar

      A trade-off to understand: a shorter flattening window removes more red noise, but it also eats long dips. Version 1 of our detector used a 1-day window and lost real transits. Version 2 uses 2 days (Chapter 8).

      7.5 Worked example: one star, four noise numbers¶

      Kepler-16, sector 82, one hour of data, measured on your PC VERIFIED (and in Lab 3):

      Method One-hour noise
      lightkurve's quick estimate (flattens with a 3.4-hour window) 229 ppm
      NASA's own estimate, stored in the file header ("CDPP") 542 ppm
      our binned scatter after a 0.25-day flatten 573 ppm
      our binned scatter after a 2-day flatten (what our detector sees) 908 ppm
      what white noise would give (Eq. 7.2) 368 ppm

      Same data, a factor of 4 apart. The quick estimate flattens so hard that it removes most of the slow wobble. It even lands below the white-noise value, which is a warning sign by itself. Our own 19 October report used that quick number (236 ppm), and it fed an early claim that we could find planets of 2 to 5 Earth radii. Injection tests later measured the real floor: about 6 VERIFIED. Corrected and logged.

      The lesson: a noise number means nothing without the method. Quote the one that matches how your search sees the data, and check it against the file's own CDPP.

      7.6 What this lets us conclude, and what it doesn't¶

      • We can: measure the noise our search actually faces, and set a bar whose false-alarm rate we've measured (upside-down data).
      • We can't assume bell-curve maths. 7σ is not "one in 780 billion" in real TESS data.
      • Not yet known (not tested): how much of Kepler-16's extra hour-scale noise is the stars themselves and how much is the spacecraft. Comparing quiet and noisy sectors would tell us.
