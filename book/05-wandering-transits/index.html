      5. Why two-sun transits wander¶

      Chapter 5 · about 25 min · Maths: averages, rough speeds, percentages

      Uses the voice on your own device.

      1-minute summary

      • What: why a two-sun planet's dips come early, late, longer or shorter.
      • Why: that wandering hides them from normal search tools, which is our chance.
      • You will be able to: say why a search that expects a steady clock misses them.

      The story

      TOI-1338 b is no clock. Its first three crossings came about 93 and 95 days apart, and lasted about 0.3, 0.4 and 0.6 days VERIFIED. Worse for anyone searching, its orbit slowly tilts, so it swings in and out of view over 21.4 years VERIFIED. A planet that is late, a different shape each time and sometimes absent: that is what wandering means, and it is why a search that expects a steady beat misses it.

      In plain words

      Around one star, a planet's dips arrive like a clock. Around two moving stars they do not, which makes these planets hard to find and gives our hunt its chance.

      What this chapter answers: why are circumbinary transits irregular, and how does that create our G1 opportunity?

      5.1 The intuition¶

      Imagine a lighthouse on a boat, and the boat is circling in the harbour. You walk around the harbour on a path. Each time you pass in front of the lighthouse beam, the boat is somewhere different on its circle. Sometimes you meet it early, sometimes late. If the boat is moving the same way you are, you stay in its beam longer; if it moves the opposite way, you flash past.

      The boat is the star being crossed. You're the planet.

      With JavaScript on, this spot shows two stars circling each other and a planet circling both, with the brightness plotted underneath. The planet's crossings come at uneven gaps.

      Try it. Press Play, or drag Time. The grey line is the two stars passing in front of each other, and it keeps a steady beat. The amber line is the planet's dips, and they do not: the gaps and the lengths change, and some crossings can be missing altogether. Change the planet's year and watch the gaps change. The picture is not to scale: the planet is drawn far too big so that its dip shows, and the orbits are drawn as circles.

      5.2 The scientific meaning¶

      Three effects make circumbinary transits irregular:

      1. Timing shifts. Each star swings around the pair's centre. The planet crosses the star wherever the star happens to be, so the gaps between transits vary. These shifts can be days, longer than a transit lasts, so the usual trick of folding the data at one period fails. Every Kepler circumbinary discovery was made by eye VERIFIED.
      2. Duration changes. If the star moves the same direction as the planet, the crossing is slow and long; the opposite direction makes it short. TOI-1338 b's first three TESS transits came about 93 and 95 days apart and lasted about 0.3, 0.4 and 0.6 days VERIFIED.
      3. Switching on and off. The planet's orbit slowly tilts around (it precesses). For part of each cycle it's lined up to cross the stars; for the rest it misses them completely. Kepler-453 b crosses only about 8% of the time: for every one like it we see, about 12 are hidden right now LIKELY.

      5.3 The technical explanation¶

      Precession and transit windows¶

      The two stars aren't a perfect point mass, so they pull the planet's orbit plane round in a slow circle. Call the time for one full turn the precession period. For TOI-1338 b it's 21.4 years VERIFIED. Transits are only possible while the planet's orbit plane cuts across the stars, so they come in windows separated by long silences.

      Kostov et al. (2020) worked out that TOI-1338 b's transits would become unlikely, with at most about a 30% chance per crossing, after 12 September 2025 VERIFIED. Yet a 2024 model by Wang and Liu predicted one specific crossing on 16 December 2025, and our search found a dip that same night VERIFIED. The "30%" was a chance, not a ban: this was one of the crossings that happened.

      Why normal searches miss them¶

      NASA's SPOC pipeline flags a planet only after it sees at least two transits and a detection score above 7.1 LIKELY. Its multi-transit method assumes a fixed period. A two-sun planet with shifting timing, changing durations, and one transit per sector can slip through.

      5.4 Our implementation: the G1 idea¶

      From 2009 to 2013 the Kepler telescope stared at one patch of sky and catalogued about 2,900 eclipsing binaries. Kepler's planets were found in that window. But precession means some planets that were not crossing back then could be crossing now. A 2017 study estimated Kepler's binaries hold about 17 to 30 more such planets that will start transiting someday, possibly during TESS LIKELY.

      TESS has re-observed the Kepler field. G1 is the search of those binaries in the newer TESS data. In code this means:

      • take the Kepler binary's period and eclipse times from the catalogue;
      • mask its eclipses (Chapter 8);
      • search what's left for single dips, and later for groups of dips that fit a circumbinary orbit.

      5.5 Worked example: TOI-1338 b's timeline¶

      When What happened Source
      2018-2019 (sectors 3, 6, 10) first three transits, 93 and 95 days apart, 0.3-0.6 d long VERIFIED
      2020 planet announced; precession period 21.4 yr; transits predicted to become rare after Sep 2025 VERIFIED
      2024 Wang and Liu predict a crossing on 16 Dec 2025, 02:13 UTC, 6.31 h VERIFIED
      16 Dec 2025 our search finds a 0.24% dip that night, about 1 hour after the predicted time VERIFIED

      That one-hour difference is bigger than the paper's stated error (±23 min). It could be real wobble, or our timing error could be bigger than it looks. We haven't measured our error carefully yet (not measured yet; it's on the task list). A known planet's crossing that nobody has published yet is a nice result for practice, but it is not a discovery.

      5.6 What this lets us conclude, and what it doesn't¶

      • It explains why there's room for us: standard tools are built for clockwork planets.
      • It doesn't promise planets. The 17-30 estimate is a model, and many of those planets could be small or faint.
      • Irregular timing cuts both ways. It hides planets from NASA's pipeline, but it also means one dip alone is weak evidence. A strong case needs dips that fit a physical orbit around the two stars.
