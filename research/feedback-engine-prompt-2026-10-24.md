I need a detailed research report that a coding agent (Claude Code) will use to build a "feedback engine". Please research on the web, cite sources, and tag every claim (rules at the end).

WHO AND WHAT
I am building a free citizen-science project, "Second Sun", that hunts for planets that circle two stars (circumbinary planets) in public NASA TESS telescope data. The learner is a beginner (Ahmed), reading on an iPhone, who is training his eyes to judge short stretches of a star's brightness over time (a "light curve") before he looks at real candidates. He answers each card with Dip / No dip / Odd / Not sure; once real data starts there will be more answers (planet-like, eclipse, flare, step, instrument, unclear) plus a confidence.

THE CARDS (what the software knows)
Each card is an array of about 216 brightness values (parts per million relative to the star's normal level), one per 10 minutes, so 36 hours, with null for missing data. Typical scatter is about 1,000 ppm per point (2 to 4 times more on real, fainter stars).
Kinds: clear fake planet (flat bottom, sloping sides, 4 to 12 hours, strength 10 to 16); fake planet near the limit (strength 5 to 8.5); hard noise (the single best-looking dip that a box-shaped filter finds in a whole sector of noise-only data, strength typically 3.5 to 8.5); a real eclipse of the two stars (very deep); a spike up; a step up or down that stays; a dip that ends exactly where a gap in the data begins (a thruster-firing artifact); random gaps as decoys. The easy mode also has box-shaped fake dips and a quiet random stretch. "Strength" = depth of the best box divided by the scatter of a box average; the real hunt keeps 7 and above.
In practice and tests the truth and the injected numbers are known. LATER, in real mode, only the array and some context are known (eclipse timing of the two stars, thruster-firing times, gaps, a second data product), the truth is unknown, and an independent verdict made by Claude is sealed until he has answered.

WHAT HE SEES NOW, AND WHY IT FAILS
After each answer in practice there is a banner with a fixed headline (THIS WAS: A DIP or NOT A DIP), a one-line "what it was" taken from a per-kind sentence, a line saying right / not quite, and a note. For all hard-noise cards the text is word for word the same except one number. Every gap-dip card says the same two sentences. Every spike says "a short spike upwards, like a flare or a glitch. A planet only makes dips." He told me: "almost every noise is different but the same response is copy pasted; nothing new is learned; I just remember this is familiar to the previous noise."
His real habits (from his data): he calls even weak noise a dip, he calls wiggly noise "Odd", he rarely says Not sure, he finds all the clear fake planets, and he misses most dips that touch a gap.

WHAT I WANT
Feedback that is (1) personalised to the exact card by MEASURING it (not by looking up its label), automatically, so 500 cards need no hand-written text; (2) the same length whether he was right, wrong or unsure (about 3 short lines, about 40 words); (3) different from card to card because the measured facts differ, with rules that stop the same lesson being repeated once he shows he knows it; (4) meaningful: each sentence is a fact measured on this card, why it matters for telling a planet from noise or an artifact, and what to look for next time; (5) honest in real mode: no certainty, and no verdict before he has locked his own answer; (6) in plain words, no dashes, with a one-line gloss for any jargon.
It must run in a web browser in plain JavaScript, offline, deterministic, small, with no AI at run time, and be easy to test (the same card gives the same text; every number in the text equals a computed number).

WHAT I NEED IN YOUR REPORT (detailed, structured, for a coding agent)
1. What the research says makes feedback teach: perceptual learning with feedback, elaborated versus verdict-only feedback, timing, contrastive and near-twin cases, interleaving, desirable difficulties, when feedback hurts or becomes a crutch, radiology and other image-reading training, and how Planet Hunters, Space Warps and other Zooniverse projects train and give feedback. Say what is solid and what is weak.
2. A ranked list of 15 to 25 measurable features of a 36 hour window that separate a planet-like dip from noise, an eclipse, a flare or spike, a step, a data-gap artifact, a slow ramp or scattered light, and a variable star. For each: a precise formula I can code from the array (JavaScript-ready pseudocode), typical values for each class, the thresholds you would use, and a source or a way to calibrate by simulation. Include at least: strength, duration, depth in noise units, the gap between the best and second-best wiggle, edge steepness (ingress time), flat-bottom versus V shape, rebound after the event, symmetry, distance from the nearest gap or data edge, red-noise level, number of points inside the event, and how many other events of similar strength are in the same card.
3. The feedback grammar: the slots (for example measurement, meaning, your answer), a selection rule that picks which features to mention (diagnostic value, novelty, the learner's mistake), anti-repetition and fading rules, and the length control. Show it as a small decision table.
4. At least 40 worked examples: feature values in, three-line text out, covering every card kind times right, wrong and unsure, all of the same length. Include examples for his habits above.
5. An adaptive layer: what to track per learner (confusion counts by feature band and card kind, streaks), when to mention a pattern ("in this round 4 of your 5 false alarms were..."), and how to fade a lesson he has mastered.
6. Visual overlays for the reveal after he answers (baseline band, best box, second-best wiggle, a ghost planet of the same strength) and what the research says about whether they help, plus phone-screen cautions.
7. How to extend the same engine to real data (truth unknown, extra panels, a sealed second opinion) without anchoring him.
8. How to test the engine, and how to measure whether the feedback actually improves his accuracy (held-out cards, a within-learner comparison), and the main pitfalls (false precision, learning the generator's quirks, overload on a phone, feedback that is too clever).

Tag every claim: [READ] a primary source you opened, [READ-2] a secondary page, [MEMORY] from training, not opened, [INFERRED] your own reasoning or recommendation. End with "What I could not verify" and a source list with links. Where you are unsure, say so. Starting points I remember (verify, do not trust): Hattie and Timperley 2007 on feedback, Shute 2008 on formative feedback, Kluger and DeNisi 1996 on feedback interventions, Ahissar and Hochstein on perceptual learning, Marshall et al. 2016 (Space Warps) and Eisner et al. 2021 (Planet Hunters TESS) on training with simulated objects.
