Ahmed, this is the second lesson, written overnight while you slept. It is in the same plain voice as the first. Nothing here needs a decision from you; read it when you have ten quiet minutes. Everything I quote has a note behind it in the project, and I mark how sure I am in words.

Start with the oddest thing I found. When I counted how many planets our three searches should have found if circumbinary planets are as common as the Kepler telescope said, the answer on paper was between thirteen and twenty-two. We found none. If that were true, our silence would be one chance in a million, and I would have had to tell you either that planets around our kind of binary are almost absent or that something in our own calculations is broken. It was the second. The map I built to say what our search could see counted the chance that a transit falls inside the days TESS watched, but it quietly assumed that a planet in a binary's orbital plane always crosses the face of a star. It does not. A planet circling two stars at, say, five times the stars' own separation crosses a star only if the whole system is lined up almost exactly edge-on, and a binary that eclipses is only roughly edge-on. When I put that geometry in, star by star, using each binary's real eclipse width, the factor came out between about five and twenty per cent of what I had assumed. With it, our three searches together should have found about one to four planets at Kepler's rate, and the chance of finding none was somewhere between three and fifty per cent (a few degrees of tilt widen the range: in the most optimistic corner the chance of none falls to about one per cent, so the honest summary is a range, not a verdict). So the honest reading of our three empty searches is not "there are no planets" and not even "planets are rarer here". It is "we have not yet looked hard enough to tell". That is a weaker statement than the one we were quietly hoping to make, but it is true, and it tells us exactly how much more looking is worth doing.

I want to be straight about how solid this is. The formula I used is from a published paper, Martin and Triaud in 2015, and I read the equation myself. The step that turns it into a yes-or-no for each star is my own reasoning from their equation, and I have not yet checked it against the planets Kepler already found; the number also depends on how big the second star in each binary is, which I had to assume. So call the five to twenty per cent likely, not confirmed. Also, a few degrees of tilt between the planet's orbit and the binary's would make planets cross the stars more often, so my number is more a floor than a ceiling. The fix, which I have filed as task T138, is to do this properly and then re-run all three maps. That needs your yes because it touches a rule we locked before any result.

Now the part you asked me to teach from the basics: what Census can do about it. A search is only as good as the number of "star-equivalents" it holds: add up, over every star you looked at, the chance that a planet of a given size and period would have shown. Six hundred bright stars with many sectors can be worth more than a thousand faint ones. G2, the bright search, was worth roughly four to five times G3 per star for a planet the size of Kepler-16 b (87 against about 19 star-equivalents for about the same number of stars). That is why I keep saying the number of stars is the wrong ruler. Census would scan about 259,000 bright stars that TESS watched at two-minute cadence after sector 26 and that no published list looks at. Only about four stars in a thousand of those are the kind we want, a binary that is bright, detached and takes more than five days to go round, so the usable list is about 1,400 stars, not 259,000. Those stars would mostly have two or three sectors each, which makes each worth about half of a G2 star. Even so, adding them to what we have gives all our searches together something like two to five and a half expected planets at Kepler's rate, a chance of at least one detection between about 87 and 100 per cent, and, if we found nothing, a cap on the total rate of roughly five to fifteen per cent. In plain words, Census would be the first search of ours that could settle the question one way or the other, as long as the geometry factor is near what I think it is.

One more thing I checked, because you asked whether the work is just too big. The 21 hours on the clock for G3's download were not about the size of the files. About 20 of them were a stall after a server time-out that nobody noticed; the restart finished the last third in ten minutes. I had told you earlier it took over a day, then 21 hours; the honest version is about an hour and a quarter of real work. I tested a public Amazon bucket that holds the same light curves and got about three files a second with eight parallel streams, at 1.8 megabytes a file. Scanning all 259,000 stars once, one sector each, is about 23 hours and 460 gigabytes, and every sector of every star about 46 hours and 910 gigabytes, which we can process and delete as we go. I only tested 66 files, so I would not promise the speed for hours on end, but it means the list is a days-long job, not a months-long one. The hard work is the finder and the vetting, and I have not started either.

I should also correct two things I said earlier. I told you the safe-window problem cost us 14 per cent of the reads; it is 16 per cent of the star-sectors, and it is concentrated exactly where no binary list exists, 27 per cent in sectors 27 to 55, and searching them would only have added eight per cent to G3's reach. And I told you Census idea 4, linking dips that fit one orbit, might be original. It is not: Martin and Fabrycky's STANLEY and Windemuth and colleagues already stack and link transits on Kepler data. What I could not find is that done on TESS single events across sectors with an upside-down control, so that narrow combination may still be ours, but I will not say so until a wider search is done.
