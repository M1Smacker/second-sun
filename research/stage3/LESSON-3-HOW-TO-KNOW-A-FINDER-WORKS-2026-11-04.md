## Lesson 3: how a machine finds eclipsing binaries, and how we would know ours works

Ahmed, this one is for the day you decide to start stage 2, so you can judge the proposal instead of trusting it. It is in the same plain voice. Nothing here is built; it is the thinking that comes before building.

Start with what the machine is looking for. An eclipsing binary is two stars that cross in front of each other as seen from here, so the light dips twice per orbit, a deep dip when the hotter star is covered and a shallower one when it is the other way round. For a binary that goes round in half a day, a single TESS sector holds fifty or more pairs of dips, and almost any method finds them: the dips repeat so often that you can fold them on top of each other and they stack into one obvious shape. That is why the published lists are full of short-period binaries. Our targets are the opposite. A binary with a period of ten days shows at most two or three eclipses in a 27-day sector, and one with a period of forty days may show one or none. The planets we want only live around such wide binaries, so our finder has to work on very few dips, which is a much harder job than counting hundreds.

There are three honest ways to do it, and you should know their trade-offs. The first is the old box search: slide a box shape along the light curve over many trial periods, fold the data at each period and keep the period where the dip stacks best. It is cheap and well understood, and it is the right tool when there are at least three eclipses, but with one or two eclipses it fits noise as easily as binaries. The second is a single-event search: look for any dip that is deep, has a plausible shape and lasts a plausible time, then link the dips of the same star across sectors by asking whether their depths and widths agree and whether a common period could place them. It copes with sparse data and it is how I would start, but it makes more false alarms, because many things dip once: a flare shadow is not one, but a sunspot-covered rotation, a pulsation beat, a bad stretch of spacecraft data or a nearby star's eclipse that leaks into the aperture all can. The third is a trained neural network, which is what the big published catalogs use. It can be very good, but it needs thousands of labelled examples, it hides why it decided, and we would have no way to explain a miss to you. I would not start there.

Whichever we pick, the question you should ask is not whether it finds binaries, because every method finds some, but how we would know how many it misses and how many fakes it lets in. This is the same idea as the planted fake planets in the main search, only for binaries. We already own the answer key: 4,161 binaries from the published list were observed again after sector 26, and 1,036 of them are the long-period, detached, bright kind we care about. A fair test is to fetch a few hundred of them with a few hundred ordinary stars that are not binaries, hide the labels from the finder, run it, and then open the labels. Two numbers come out. Recall is the share of the known binaries it found, and we want to see it by period, because I expect it to be excellent below five days and weak above twenty. The false-alarm rate is the share of ordinary stars it wrongly called binaries, and we want to see which kinds of ordinary star fooled it. If recall at ten to forty days is poor, we know before we spend a day downloading that the Census list will lack exactly the binaries we most want, and we can fix the finder first.

There is one trap I want you to see now, because it caught the first search I did. A finder tested on the same stars it was designed on will always look good. The honest way is the one you already know from G2: build it on one set, freeze it, and only then score it on a set it never saw. So the test would be split in two, and the second half stays sealed until I have written down the pass marks. I would also keep the rule you gave me: no change to the finder after seeing the sealed result.

What I do not know yet, and will say so when it comes up, is how well any of this works at the faint end and for binaries whose eclipses are very shallow, because the answer key mostly holds the clear, deep cases. The published list was itself made from the easier cases, so a finder that matches it perfectly could still miss a whole class of binaries the list never contained. That is a real limit of any check against an old list, and I would put it in the report in plain words rather than hope nobody notices.

## Ideas

1. If you choose stage 2, start with the answer-key test described above, split in two halves, with pass marks written first.
2. Start with the single-event method plus linking, and keep the box search as a cross-check for the stars with many sectors.
3. Park the neural network for now; revisit it only if the first two cannot reach acceptable recall at ten to forty days.
