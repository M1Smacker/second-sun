# What the first real search taught us, and how to improve the filter (27 Oct 2026)

Plain facts, no planet claims. Real events stay in `data\` (git-ignored); only totals are here.

## 1. What happened
- **v1** (settings hash 04e917e5a7e9): 549 stars, 5,082 star-sector groups, about 30 minutes, 0 errors. 604 real dips; 186 passed the checks (tier 1); the same search on upside-down copies of the curves gave 174.
- **v2** (hash 14c896421028): same search code, same 2,642 events (checked: identical), two new flags. Real tier 1 fell to 64 (11 of them confirmed by a second data product); upside-down tier 1 fell to 90 (37 confirmed).
- **Reading:** after the fixes the real count (64) is below the control (90). There is no excess of real dips over false alarms. The control is an upper bound, because stellar flares point up and become dips when flipped, so real below control is expected for noise alone. We cannot claim a signal.

## 2. The two fixes (flags, never deletions), and why they are physics and not tuning
1. **Eclipse overlap by physical length.** An event is flagged if its window touches an eclipse of length T = P/pi x (R1+R2)/a, with a from Kepler's third law (total mass 2 Msun, R1+R2 taken as 2 x the TIC radius, never longer than 0.4 of the orbit). True length is known to about a factor of 2; the rule is a flag so that is acceptable. Stars with no TIC radius (42 of 552 rows) keep only the old 0.35 d rule. Effect: 147 real and 137 upside-down events flagged. The two strongest events of v1 (signal to noise 39 and 15) were the sloping edges of long eclipses and are now flagged.
2. **Repeater flag: 3 or more events of the same kind in one product and sector.** A planet orbiting a binary must be outside the stability limit. Holman and Wiegert's fit gives about 2.4 binary separations for a circular, equal-mass binary, so (by my own conversion, Kepler's third law) a period of about 3.7 binary periods; with our binary periods above 5 d that is about 18 d, so at most 2 transits fit in a 27 d sector (for a very unequal pair the limit drops to about 14 d, still at most 2 in nearly all cases). A third dip in one sector means a variable star or a second eclipsing pair. 226 real and 281 upside-down events flagged; 23 stars carry repeaters among the real events.
- **Honest limit:** I chose both rules after reading the v1 results (I had seen the events they remove). They are fixed by physics and applied identically to the real and the upside-down curves, so the comparison stays fair, but this is not a blind pre-registration. v2 is the new frozen baseline; do not tune it further on these 549 stars.
- Tests: 3 new checks, mutation-tested (a wrong eclipse factor and a wrong repeat count are both caught); the Stage 2 regression (36 and 84 events, published waterfall) is unchanged.

## 3. What the catalogues said (downloaded 27 Oct; files in `data\xmatch`, not in git)
- **NASA Exoplanet Archive, TESS Objects of Interest:** 6 of our 549 stars are TOIs: 5 are marked known planet and 1 is a planet candidate (TOI-7910.01, period 77.5 d, depth about 1,360 ppm, 10 h). One of the 8 strongest v1 events sat on a star that is itself a TOI (TOI-7858.01, disposition KP, which NASA defines as "known planet") whose period, 12.7137939 d, equals the star's Kepler binary period (12.713794 d); its depth is 66,413 ppm and the NASA Exoplanet Archive lists no planet for it. Why it carries a planet-type label is UNCERTAIN (likely an eclipsing binary flagged as known; ExoFOP not checked).
- **Known circumbinary planets (archive flag cb_flag=1, 53 rows):** 6 hosts are in our list: Kepler-16, 38, 453, 1647, PH1 and Kepler-1660. We used 5 as controls; **Kepler-1660 is a sixth control we have not used.**
- **TESS eclipsing binary catalogue (Prsa et al. 2022, VizieR):** 53 of our stars are in it. For 40 the TESS period matches Kepler's, for 8 it is half (equal-depth eclipses), for 1 double, and 4 disagree (possible blends). The repeater-flagged stars are almost all not in that catalogue (4 of 68), so the repeater flag is finding things that catalogue does not list, or things that appear only in later sectors.

## 4. What the literature says about the same problems
- **Davies et al. 2026** (mono-cbp, arXiv 2604.09435): after automatic vetting they still found, among 1,647 flagged events, 258 detrending artefacts and 135 systematics by eye; their residual false positives are uneliminated secondary eclipses, background variable stars and leftover stellar variability. Same classes as ours. They recover at least 50% of 14 known planets by injection.
- **LEO-Vetter** (arXiv 2509.10619): the standard TESS vetting list is shape and significance tests (momentum dumps, scattered light ramps at the start of each orbit), odd and even depth, secondary events, depths too deep for a planet, then pixel-level tests for blends.
- **Pterodactyls** (arXiv 2206.03989): flags signals whose single-event depths scatter widely and whose duration does not match the expected one, because variability makes dips longer than a transit.
- **Blend tools** (TRICERATOPS arXiv 2002.00691, DEATHSTAR arXiv 2312.08373): need pixel and neighbour data; only worth running on survivors.

## 5. How the filter can improve next (ranked; none built)
1. **Step and ramp test (flat baseline).** A transit is a box: flux before and after should match. Five of the 8 strongest v1 events still pass v2: four look like slow drifts or orbit-start ramps (which this test targets) and one is a weak 823 ppm dip. This is the biggest cheap gain. Cost: reading event windows once (about 1,500 events).
2. **Orbit-start ramp flag:** widen the gap-edge flag to about a day after each orbit start (LEO-Vetter names this failure).
3. **Per-event depth scatter and odd/even depth (Pterodactyls and LEO-Vetter style),** applied to repeaters: a repeating, consistent pattern is a periodic blend and can be labelled instead of discarded.
4. **Use the sixth control (Kepler-1660)** and measure recovery of known planets and of injected dips under v2, per class, so every cut has a measured cost in real dips lost.
5. **Label events on stars that are TOIs, known hosts or in the TESS EB catalogue** automatically, so nobody spends time on a known object.
6. **Judge every future version by real minus upside-down per tier,** and by recovery of injections, not by list length.
7. **Pixel and centroid tests (blends)** only for survivors, later (needs target pixel files).
8. **Pair search** (two dips close together) was never built; it is still a gap and a decision for Ahmed.

## 6. What this does not tell us
No human has looked at any v2 event. Nothing here is a candidate. (The step test was built later that evening as v3, see section 7.)

## 7. Second look: my doubts, checked against data and the web (27 Oct, while v3 ran)
1. **Is the upside-down control trustworthy? Partly.** Kepler's own team used inverted and scrambled light curves to measure false alarms ([arXiv:1906.03575](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.03575), [arXiv:1710.06758](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1710.06758)), so the method is standard. But in Kepler the two controls disagreed by about a factor of 2 (for periods of 200 to 500 d and low signal, inverted-only gave a reliability of 24%, scrambled-only 51%, a random mix 38% +/- 8%; CONFIRMED in the paper's section VII.3.3). One control is one view. **Add a scrambled control** (shuffle the light curve in blocks) and report both.
2. **Our signal-to-noise is probably inflated by correlated noise. VERIFIED by numbers.** Kepler's 7.1 sigma bar is a Gaussian false alarm probability of about 6.28 x 10^-13 under a Neyman-Pearson criterion, and Kepler's pipeline adds a per-target bootstrap correction "so that we can ensure the required false alarm rate is met" ([arXiv:1501.03586](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1501.03586), CONFIRMED in the text). The claim I first wrote here, about 1.7 x 10^7 trials per star and one false alarm per mission, came from a search summary and is UNCERTAIN: I did not find it in the part of the paper I read. Our upside-down control found 869 events, so our noise is far from Gaussian (my estimate of about 5 million independent trials in our search gives well under 1 false alarm for Gaussian noise: LIKELY, the trial count is mine). The Kepler per-target bootstrap is the model for fixing this. Raising the bar does not help: real over upside-down is 0.69 at SNR 7, 0.72 at 12, 0.78 at 15 for all events, and for unflagged tier 1 it is 0.71, 0.54 and 1.0 (4 of 4, too few). Real stays at or below the control at every threshold. **Next fix: a red-noise-corrected SNR.**
3. **The duration grid piles events at its two ends.** Events by box length (real | upside-down): 3 h 176 | 212, and 15 h 142 | 168, against 30 to 60 for the middle lengths. The shortest box catches white-noise spikes and the longest catches slow trends. Nothing longer than 15 h is searched, yet long circumbinary transits can run longer. Both a noise magnet and a blind spot.
4. **Detrending window: doubt resolved.** The detector flattens with a 2 day biweight window. Wotan's guidance is a window of about 3 times the transit length, at least about 2.2 ([arXiv:1906.00966](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.00966)), so 2 days is right up to about 15 h (the longest box). A 2 day window leaves slower ramps in, which matches what we saw.
5. **My eclipse-length formula over-flags. VERIFIED against ground truth.** On the 30 stars whose primary eclipse width is measured in the TESS eclipsing-binary catalogue (same period as Kepler, TIC radius known), my predicted length was 1.5 to 6.4 times the measured width (median 2.7). It covered the whole measured eclipse in all 30, so no eclipse leaks, but it is too generous. Caveat: I did not confirm whether the catalogue's width is contact-to-contact or half-depth, so some of the 2.7 may be definition. Cost of the flag: it covers a median 12% of the timeline (90th percentile 26%; 27 of 508 stars above 30%). **For a later version, use the measured width where the catalogue has one (53 stars) and fall back to the formula.** v2 and v3 stay frozen.
6. **Still not measured:** recovery of real known planets (Kepler-1660 and the other controls), a pair search, and red-noise corrected significance.

## 8. v3 result: the flat-baseline step test (hash 23a74c021d8a, frozen; the search output is identical to v2, 2,642 events)
- **Cost on real-shaped dips, measured:** injected 4, 8 and 14 h boxes into 160 random star-sectors: 97.7% of detected injections are kept; the 2.3% flagged all lacked data on one side, none had a baseline lift (`step_retention.txt`). Caveat: injections sit in the middle of a data segment, so this does not measure planets that transit near a segment edge, which no flat-baseline test can verify.
- **Tier 1 after v3:** real 15 (2 confirmed by a second product), upside-down 38 (7 confirmed). Real minus upside-down is -23 for tier 1, -5 for tier 1A, and stays negative at every signal-to-noise bar from 7 to 12 (-23, -18, -11, -6). From v1 to v3 real tier 1 went 186 to 64 to 15; the control went 174 to 90 to 38.
- **Surprise, verified:** the step flag fired on 400 of 604 real events (305 of those only because one side has no data) and 633 of 869 control events (510). So more than half of everything the detector finds sits at the edge of a data segment (orbit starts, gaps): that is where the false alarms live. The flag removed real events faster than control events, so the real events were even more concentrated at edges and ramps than the control.
- **The first 8 events:** 6 are now flagged: all 6 by the step test, 3 of them also by eclipse overlap. The 2 left are a weak 823 ppm dip and one messy drift; neither is a candidate.
- **Reading:** after the first version (real 186 against upside-down 174 in tier 1, the extra real dips being mostly repeater stars and eclipse edges that later flags removed) the real curves never beat the upside-down control, at any version or threshold, so the search finds no sign of planets at this detection level. That is a result, not a failure: it bounds what this detector can do on these stars and says the next gains are in noise handling (FIX-QUEUE item 5), not in more flags.

## 11. v4 (hash d5767e66c4d2, 28 Oct 2026): a correction to my own diagnosis, and the result
- **Correction (CONFIRMED by reading the code).** Section 7 item 2 said our signal-to-noise is "probably inflated by correlated noise". Wrong as stated: `detect_v2.box_scores` already divides by the measured scatter of the box statistic itself (1.4826 x the median absolute deviation of the box means across all trial centres, per duration, per light curve), which includes correlated noise in the bulk. What is left is heavy tails and noise that changes along a sector (flares, ramps), which a single sector-wide sigma cannot describe. So the planned "red-noise-corrected SNR" is replaced by a local calibration, and FIX-QUEUE item 5 is rewritten.
- **What v4 added (flags only; the search output is identical to v3, 2,642 events):** (1) *dominated*: an event is flagged if the strongest event of the other kind (real or upside-down) in the same light curve is at least as strong; symmetric, no setting; (2) *ramp*: within 1 day after data resumes (it costs 14.2% of the data timeline, measured on 160 random star-sectors); (3) *measured eclipse widths* from the TESS eclipsing binary catalogue for the 40 stars whose periods agree, else the physics formula.
- **Completeness ceiling of the computable flags on injected dips (1,548 injections, 4 to 14 h, box and trapezoid, deep): 82.7%** before the ramp flag (about 71% if transit times are uniform over the ramp's 14%); gap, burst, repeater and second-product rules are not in that number; faint dips NOT TESTED. See `step_retention.txt`.
- **Result.** Tier 1 real 12, upside-down 33 (excess -21); without the ramp flag 14 against 37 (-23). From v3 the real count fell 15 to 12 and the control 38 to 33, so the new flags change little: most events were already flagged. Real stays below the control at every SNR bar (7, 8, 10, 12: 12/33, 9/24, 4/13, 2/6). The 12 real tier-1 events are on 10 stars (10 QLP, 1 SPOC, 1 TESS-SPOC), none on a TOI, known host or catalogued eclipsing binary.
- **A new clue (LIKELY, NOT TESTED).** The dominated flag hits 134 of 604 real events (22%) but 111 of 869 upside-down events (13%): the strongest event in a light curve is more often an upside-down one. That fits positive excursions (flares, upward systematics) being larger than negative ones, so the upside-down control probably OVERSTATES false alarms for dips. Then "real below control" is expected from noise alone and says nothing in either direction. The honest bound is that no signal is visible, not that real is below noise. A scrambled control (FIX-QUEUE item 6) is the test and moves to the top of v5.

## 12. The asymmetry test, and the scrambled control withdrawn (28 Oct 2026)
- **Scrambled control withdrawn (CONFIRMED by reading the source).** Kepler's scrambling moved whole one-year blocks and kept the order inside each block, so it destroys repeating timing but not a single dip ([arXiv:1710.06758](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1710.06758)). Our search looks for single dips, so a scrambled control is not a null for it. FIX-QUEUE item 6 is replaced by the measurement below.
- **Asymmetry test (`asymmetry.txt`, 900 random star-sectors, all products, raw dips with no flags), CONFIRMED as a measurement.** Upside-down over real: 1.25 at SNR 4.5, 1.34 at 5, 1.77 at 6, 2.30 at 7, 2.52 at 8, 2.07 at 10. The upside-down curve finds more dips at every level and the gap grows with strength. Planets would add dips to the real side and pull the ratio down; it rises, so there is no excess of real dips at any level. LIKELY (not tested): the cause is upward spikes (flares, upward systematics). So the upside-down control overstates false alarms for dips by roughly a factor of 1.3 to 2.5, and "real below control" is expected from noise alone.
- **A correction.** Earlier notes (section 8 and the status table) said the real curves never beat the control. That is wrong for version 1: tier 1 was real 186 against 174, a small excess made mostly of repeater stars and eclipse edges that later flags removed (v1 to v2: 122 real and 84 upside-down tier-1 events moved to flagged, mostly by the repeater and overlap flags). From version 2 on real is below the control. Both lines are corrected above.
- **Rough chance that a findable planet is still hiding in the 549 stars: about 1 in 10 (range roughly 3 to 20 in 100), LIKELY, my own arithmetic.** Prior from the 24 Oct yield model: expected detectable planets 0.31 to 0.42, so 27% to 34% that at least one exists; our measured keep rate for strong dips is about 0.7; so P(exists and missed | none found) = 0.27 x 0.3 / (1 - 0.27 x 0.7) = 10% to 0.34 x 0.3 / (1 - 0.34 x 0.7) = 13%. It ignores faint planets (untested), the 12 survivors (nobody has looked at them) and pair searches (not built).

## 14. Pair search, first version (28 Oct 2026, `pipeline/pair_search.py`, `pair_search_v5.txt`)
- **What it does.** On the events of the frozen v5 run (hash 5392f864e810, nothing in the detector changed), it links dips of one star that sit in different sectors and have depths within a factor of 1.5 and durations within a factor of 1.5. Rules were fixed before any result was read. The same rules run on the upside-down copies. Aggregates only; the unit of the noise line is stars with a pair.
- **Result (CONFIRMED, our own run).** Clean class (tier 1A, 1L, 1B, best product): real 14 dips on 12 stars, **0 pairs**; upside-down 35 dips on 33 stars, 2 pairs. Noise alone would give 0.32 real stars with a pair. - **Two things I changed after the first print, on purpose and stated here.** The first print used every product's version of a dip and counted pairs: it showed 24 clean real dips with 10 real pairs on 2 stars, against 2 upside-down pairs on 2 stars. - **What this cannot say (NOT TESTED).** (a) The frozen run keeps only dips at SNR 7 or more, so a pair of two weaker dips (SNR 5 to 7) is invisible; that needs a re-search and a new freeze. (b) No alias check: for a real pair, every shorter period Dt/n must put its other transits into data gaps. The group files do not store time stamps, so that needs a second pass over the light curves. (c) Circumbinary transits are not strictly periodic, so Dt/n is only a first filter. (d) With 14 clean real dips on 12 stars, the number of possible pairs is tiny; the pair search can only get interesting after a lower bar.
