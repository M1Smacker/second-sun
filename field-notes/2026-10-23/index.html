    23 October 2026: rebuilding the book around a map¶

    Field note · day 5 · about 8 min

    In plain words

    You told me the book forced labs on you, had no overview and felt disconnected, so we rebuilt it. The home page is now a map of the real sky, with three tabs, optional labs, one review page per Part and a summary at the top of every chapter. Nothing about the science changed, and the star hunt is still on hold.

    What we did, and why¶

    1. You answered 19 rounds of questions, and I wrote them into a design brief (research/BOOK-REDESIGN-BRIEF.md). You approved it and chose font B, Inter.
    2. The home page is a map of the real sky around Cygnus. The six large stars are the six Parts and the small dots around each are its chapters. Behind them are 310 real stars, the Milky Way, a chart grid, and 546 amber specks: our Kepler stars, each at its true place. (Three of our 549 have no position in the catalogue, so the caption says "546 drawn".)
    3. Three tabs, one flat list. The menu is Read, Hunt and Reference. Under Read, the fifteen chapters sit in one list with the Part names as dividers.
    4. Two ways through the same pages. The journey follows the order the project needed each idea; by topic is the textbook order. A button at the end of every chapter switches between them.
    5. Every chapter now opens the same way: a strip showing where you are, a three-line 1-minute summary, then a two-sentence plain-words box. It ends with Mark as read and a Next link that says why you might want it.
    6. Labs are optional. They moved to a "Try it yourself" page at the end of each Part, with the real output first and the commands in a fold. The chapters no longer send you to them.
    7. Questions moved too. The 48 chapter questions now live on one review page per Part ("Check yourself"), where nothing is scored or saved.
    8. Glossary words are tappable. The first time a chapter uses a term, it links to a small popup with the plain definition.
    9. A new look: the Inter font, hosted in the book so it works offline, and text colours made to a 7-to-1 contrast or better. A test now fails if the colours slip below that.
    10. Every chapter opens with a short story, in the personal voice, built only from facts already in the register (Kepler-16, TOI-1338 b and our own days), each with its label. The story of PH1 b waits until its facts are checked.
    11. Two tap-and-drag demos. In Chapter 5, two stars and a planet circle in a top-down view while a light curve is drawn beneath: set the planet's year to the real one (6.52 orbits of the stars) and it says how far apart the crossings land compared with how long each lasts. In Chapter 7, a made-up light curve with a hidden dip lets you turn up the noise, average points together and switch on a slow drift to see why averaging stops helping.

    The idea behind it¶

    A map is a promise about where things are. To draw part of the sky on a flat page you need each star's position (right ascension and declination, like longitude and latitude on the sky) and a rule for flattening a sphere. We used a gnomonic projection, which keeps straight lines straight and distorts only a little over a patch this size. Brightness becomes dot size: a smaller magnitude number means a brighter star, so it gets a bigger dot. Colour comes from the star's spectral type. That is why the map is real: change the catalogue and the picture changes.

    How you could do it yourself¶

    Open book/data/path.yml: it lists the Parts, the fifteen chapters, their summaries and the journey order. Edit a line, then run .venv-docs\Scripts\python.exe -m mkdocs build --strict inside book, and the map, the strips and the Next links all follow. To redraw the sky from fresh catalogues, book/tools/make_sky.py (needs the internet) rewrites book/data/sky.json.

    What we found, and what we didn't¶

    • Tested: the strict build passes; a link checker followed every link and anchor on all 61 built pages; the colour test passes; the home page does not scroll sideways at phone width; the glossary popup, Mark as read and the reading-order switch all worked in the browser.
    • Not done yet: the light-curve explorer demo (needs a real light curve saved in the book), eyes mode, one diagram per chapter, cheat sheets and audio. They are the next phases in the brief.
    • Not tested: you have not read it yet. The real test is your 15 minutes: tell me where you got lost and that goes to the top of the fix list.

    Later the same night: breaking it on purpose¶

    After the build I tried to break the book, which is where the real lessons were.

    • A tool crashed on our map. The theme rewrites every link on a page when you move between pages, and that code throws an error on links inside an SVG picture, because an SVG link's address is read-only. A screenshot would never show it; a browser test that listens for errors did. The map's links now use the older xlink:href form, and the error is gone.
    • Sizes need a ruler, not an eye. A script measured every tappable thing at phone width. The map's dots were about 15 pixels wide, too small for a thumb, and several links were short. They are now at least 24 pixels (the map) or 44 (everything else), and the check runs before every publish.
    • Facts need their labels even in summaries. The new summaries repeated numbers (14 planets, one in four candidates) without the little VERIFIED chips. They have them now.
    • A rule only counts if you have seen it refuse. Ten deliberately broken copies (a missing chapter, a sidebar that disagrees with the page order, a story that is too long) are each refused by book/tools/test_shape.py. A page nobody links to, a stray dash and a colour that fails 7-to-1 are also caught.

    Idea: test the failure, not the happy path. A check you have never seen fail is a hope, not a check.

    You could try it: in book/data/path.yml, change one chapter's part and run the build. It stops with a plain message instead of publishing a book whose map and menu disagree.

    Questions¶

    1. Why do bigger dots mean brighter stars on the map?

    Magnitude is backwards: a smaller number is brighter. The map turns that into a dot size, so the brightest stars (like Deneb) are drawn largest.

    2. Why does the caption say 546 drawn, not 549?

    Three of our stars have no sky position in the Villanova Kepler catalogue, and we only draw what we can place. The book says so rather than rounding up.
