    23 October 2026: rebuilding the book around a map¶

    Field note · day 5 · about 8 min

    In plain words

    You told me the book forced labs on you, had no overview and felt disconnected, so we rebuilt it. The home page is now a map of the real sky, with three tabs, optional labs, one review page per Part and a summary at the top of every chapter. Nothing about the science changed, and the star hunt is still on hold.

    What we did, and why¶

    1. You answered 19 rounds of questions, and I wrote them into a design brief (research/BOOK-REDESIGN-BRIEF.md). You approved it and chose font B, Inter.
    2. The home page is a map of the real sky around Cygnus. The six large stars are the six Parts and the small dots around each are its chapters. Behind them are 310 real stars, the Milky Way, a chart grid, and 546 amber specks: our Kepler stars, each at its true place. (Three of our 549 have no position in the catalogue, so the caption says "546 drawn".)
    3. Three tabs, one flat list. The menu is Read, Hunt and Reference. Under Read, the fifteen chapters sit in one list with the Part names as dividers.
    4. Two ways through the same pages. The journey follows the order the project needed each idea; by topic is the textbook order. A button at the end of every chapter switches between them.
    5. Every chapter now opens the same way: a strip showing where you are, a three-line 1-minute summary, then a two-sentence plain-words box. It ends with Mark as read and a Next link that says why you might want it.
    6. Labs are optional. They moved to a "Try it yourself" page at the end of each Part, with the real output first and the commands in a fold. The chapters no longer send you to them.
    7. Questions moved too. The 48 chapter questions now live on one review page per Part ("Check yourself"), where nothing is scored or saved.
    8. Glossary words are tappable. The first time a chapter uses a term, it links to a small popup with the plain definition.
    9. A new look: the Inter font, hosted in the book so it works offline, and text colours made to a 7-to-1 contrast or better. A test now fails if the colours slip below that.
    10. Every chapter opens with a short story, in the personal voice, built only from facts already in the register (Kepler-16, TOI-1338 b and our own days), each with its label. The story of PH1 b waits until its facts are checked.
    11. Two tap-and-drag demos. In Chapter 5, two stars and a planet circle in a top-down view while a light curve is drawn beneath: set the planet's year to the real one (6.52 orbits of the stars) and it says how far apart the crossings land compared with how long each lasts. In Chapter 7, a made-up light curve with a hidden dip lets you turn up the noise, average points together and switch on a slow drift to see why averaging stops helping. In Chapter 2, the real TOI-1338 light curve from sector 98 (10-minute averages) can be dragged and zoomed, from the whole sector down to the 0.2% dip of the planet's crossing.
    12. One small diagram tops every chapter, drawn in the book's own colours (they switch with day and night): one star with three catalogue numbers, a light curve with two kinds of dip, a funnel of dips and tests, and so on.
    13. Credited NASA pictures open Parts 1 to 3 (the Kepler and K2 fields of view, and two artist's concepts of Kepler-16b, each labelled as a drawing and credited). Parts 4 to 6 have no picture yet.
    14. The story of PH1 b, the planet that volunteers found by eye, is on the new Eyes mode page. Its facts come from the paper's abstract (claim F-059, marked LIKELY because only the abstract was read).
    15. A cheat sheet for every Part: one printable page built from the chapters, with three lines per chapter, the words used and the numbers to remember. It prints as plain black on white.
    16. A Listen button on every chapter reads it aloud with your device's own voice (free, works offline, nothing is sent anywhere). It skips pictures, code, tables and the source list, highlights what it is saying, and Pause remembers the place.
    17. Eyes mode, practice version. You see a day and a half of the real TOI-1338 light curve with nothing marked and answer Dip, No dip, Odd or Not sure; then the hidden thing is outlined. Sittings are 5, 20, 50 or 100 light curves. The stretches contain fake dips we hid, glitches, real eclipses and the real planet crossing, so the answer is always known. The real stars of the hunt are not connected: the hunt stays on hold until you say start.

    The idea behind it¶

    A map is a promise about where things are. To draw part of the sky on a flat page you need each star's position (right ascension and declination, like longitude and latitude on the sky) and a rule for flattening a sphere. We used a gnomonic projection, which keeps straight lines straight and distorts only a little over a patch this size. Brightness becomes dot size: a smaller magnitude number means a brighter star, so it gets a bigger dot. Colour comes from the star's spectral type. That is why the map is real: change the catalogue and the picture changes.

    How you could do it yourself¶

    Open book/data/path.yml: it lists the Parts, the fifteen chapters, their summaries and the journey order. Edit a line, then run .venv-docs\Scripts\python.exe -m mkdocs build --strict inside book, and the map, the strips and the Next links all follow. To redraw the sky from fresh catalogues, book/tools/make_sky.py (needs the internet) rewrites book/data/sky.json.

    • Not done yet: eyes mode on the hunt's real stars (waits for your start), a recorded voice instead of the device voice (a better-sounding tool, your call), and pictures for Parts 4 to 6. They are the next phases in the brief.
    • Not tested: the Listen button was tested with a stand-in voice in a browser with no sound, so how it sounds on your phone is unchecked. You have not read it yet. The real test is your 15 minutes: tell me where you got lost and that goes to the top of the fix list.

    Later the same night: breaking it on purpose¶

    After the build I tried to break the book, which is where the real lessons were.

    • A tool crashed on our map. The theme rewrites every link on a page when you move between pages, and that code throws an error on links inside an SVG picture, because an SVG link's address is read-only. A screenshot would never show it; a browser test that listens for errors did. The map's links now use the older xlink:href form, and the error is gone.
    • Sizes need a ruler, not an eye. A script measured every tappable thing at phone width. The map's dots were about 15 pixels wide, too small for a thumb, and several links were short. They are now at least 24 pixels (the map) or 44 (everything else), and the check runs before every publish.
    • Numbers must agree on a page. Building the explorer from the real data showed that Chapter 2 gave the primary eclipse as 5.1% and the secondary as 0.4%, while our measured values (claim F-046) are about 6% at the bottom (5.7% typical) and 0.3%. The table now uses the measured values with their label.
    • Facts need their labels even in summaries. The new summaries repeated numbers (14 planets, one in four candidates) without the little VERIFIED chips. They have them now.
    • A rule only counts if you have seen it refuse. Ten deliberately broken copies (a missing chapter, a sidebar that disagrees with the page order, a story that is too long) are each refused by book/tools/test_shape.py. A page nobody links to, a stray dash and a colour that fails 7-to-1 are also caught.

    Idea: test the failure, not the happy path. A check you have never seen fail is a hope, not a check.

    You could try it: in book/data/path.yml, change one chapter's part and run the build. It stops with a plain message instead of publishing a book whose map and menu disagree.

    Afternoon: an outside review finds two silent bugs¶

    A separate Claude, with web access and a copy of our code, answered our research brief. Everything went through Compass first: I re-ran each bug here with my own scripts, recounted its numbers from our target files and looked up all 20 of its papers in ADS. All 20 were real (Gemini's answer had 7 of 12 wrong). Full review: research/compass-reviews/2026-10-23-deep-review.md.

    • Bug 1: the detector was blind on 30-minute data. It averages the light into 10-minute bins and ignores any box less than 80% full. With one point every 30 minutes only a third of the bins have data, so every box is ignored. I hid an obvious dip (signal 23 times the noise) in fake data: found at 2, 3.3 and 10-minute spacing, nothing at 30. TESS took 30-minute pictures until 2020, and Kepler's own light curves are 30-minute, so the planned Kepler test would have failed for no real reason.
    • Bug 2: old QLP files used the wrong column. Before sector 56, QLP's flattened column has a different name (kspsap_flux), and the loader picked it. It is the same flattening we had already caught shrinking a 6-hour dip by about 70%. Our one QLP test happened to use sector 98, which has the good column, so it passed.
    • Together they touch about half of the planned QLP data. Nothing has been searched yet, so no result is wrong, but those stars would have looked quiet.
    • The odds moved up a little, to about 20% (range 5 to 45%). The outside estimate was 21 to 39%, but it used the noise of TOI-1338, a quiet star outside our list. Kepler-16, the one star of ours we have measured, is about twice as noisy. With that noise the same sum gives about 17%.
    • Timing matters: TESS looks at the Kepler field again in July and August 2027. A candidate published with a predicted date before then can be checked by TESS itself.

    Evening: the fixes, and the first look at our own stars' noise¶

    You said to apply the changes and make sure nothing breaks, so the order was: tests first, then fixes, then proof.

    • Tests that fail first. A new check hides a dip at five spacings (2, 3.3, 10, 29.4 and 30 minutes) and reads our real QLP and SPOC files. On the old code it failed at 29.4 minutes; on the new code all of it passes.
    • The fixes. The detector now averages into bins as wide as the data's own spacing when that is slower than 10 minutes. Old QLP sectors use the raw flux (it keeps the dip's depth). A file whose cleaned column is empty now falls back to the raw one instead of returning nothing.
    • Nothing else broke. The old and new detector both ran on all 51 light curves we have: 50 gave exactly the same results, and the one that changed is the old QLP file, as intended.
    • The audit, tried on 7 of our stars. In every sector it hides one standard dip and checks that it comes back: it always did where the noise allowed, and never where it did not. It also measures how much worse than ideal the noise is. The middle value is 1.76 times the ideal, and Kepler-16 is about 1.9, not the 2.8 I quoted this afternoon: that number came from a different way of measuring. So the odds lean a little up, but these 7 stars are brighter than most of ours, so I keep about 20% until all 549 are measured.
    • One early letdown, later corrected: on the one Kepler-16 sector I tried, the old QLP raw flux was about six times noisier than SPOC's. I took that as typical. When all 549 stars were measured (below), the real figure is about 1.2 times.

    Night: the Kepler test¶

    With the 30-minute bug fixed, the detector could finally read the original Kepler data. We downloaded four years of it for Kepler-16, 34 and 35 (52 files, 21 MB). No list of the exact transit times could be opened, so I found the transits a different way first, and checked each one by eye: 7 for Kepler-16, 7 for Kepler-34 and 4 for Kepler-35. Then the real detector ran blind. It found all 18, in both kinds of flux, at 13 to 320 times the noise. Most of its extra events sat at the edges of Kepler's monthly pauses, which our rules already flag for a person. One extra dip happened at the same moment on two different stars, which can only be the spacecraft. The honest limit: the transits I could see by eye are the easy ones, so this is not yet a fair race against Davies et al., who counted 9 for Kepler-34 and 7 for Kepler-35.

    Late night: you said go, in stages¶

    You approved the first two stages of the hunt: preparation, then measuring all 549 stars. Searching them is not approved yet, and nothing is searched in these stages.

    • Three small tools were added and tested. (1) Every eclipse period of a star is now masked, not only the one that passed our cut. Two stars have a second one; with it left unmasked a test star gave 175 false events, and with it masked, none. (2) An event that also appears upside down is now only flagged in the hunt, not deleted, until we have measured what that deletion loses. (3) A same-moment filter: if three or more other stars on the same camera show an event within 2.4 hours of each other, the cause is the spacecraft, so all of them are flagged for a person. My first rule (two other stars) flagged 4% of purely random events by chance, so I tightened it; chance now flags under 0.5%.
    • The download started: every light curve of every one of the 549 stars, about 8,400 files and 6 GB, saved on D: and logged one star at a time. It takes about two hours because NASA's archive answers slowly, not because of the file size.

    Then: all 549 stars measured¶

    The download finished (549 stars, 8,100 files, 5.4 GB) and the audit ran on every file. It did not search anything: in each star-sector it hides one standard dip, checks that it comes back, and records the noise. Nothing failed.

    • Our coverage table was wrong in one place. It listed 1,629 SPOC 2-minute star-sectors; the real number is 1,304. The extra 325 were multi-sector summary entries, not light curves. The star counts in the book were right.
    • The honest picture of what we can see. In a single 6.5-hour crossing, the smallest planet we could detect is below 6 Earth radii in only 196 of 5,064 star-sectors, and below 15 in 1,944. For 143 stars the best sector reaches 10 Earth radii; for 269, 15. For 239 stars (43%) no sector gets below 15, bigger than every known planet around two stars. The hidden test dip came back in 73% of sectors of stars brighter than magnitude 11, but only 8% at magnitude 12 to 13 and 1% beyond. Faint stars are close to blind.
    • The noise is about 2.5 times worse than ideal (median, from 1.7 to 4.0), not the 1.76 from the first seven stars, which were brighter than most. That is the number that decides our odds.
    • The products are closer than I feared. TESS-SPOC is as quiet as SPOC (1.05 times). Old QLP raw flux is about 1.2 times, newer QLP about 1.4 times. A star that only has QLP is not hopeless.
    • Borrowing the good stretches from neighbouring stars works: across 1,298 checks it kept 87% of each star's own good time and added almost no bad time.
    • False alarms are heavily clustered. Upside-down, the detector makes 0.17 fake events per star-sector, 869 in all, and 41% of them fall at moments when three or more other stars also show one: the spacecraft, not the stars. Our new filter flags those. Even so, about 520 fakes would be left for a person at our current bar. At a much higher bar of 12 there would still be about 110. So the next stage has to test more automatic checks, such as the same dip appearing in two independent data products.
    • The odds hardly moved: with each star's own noise, the chance of at least one real candidate comes out between 15% and 21%. I now say about 18% (range 5 to 40%).

    Idea: measure the whole population before you trust a sample. Seven bright stars said the noise was 1.76 times ideal; all 549 said 2.5.

    Idea: a filter must be tested against pure chance too. A rule that flags the real thing is not enough if it also flags 4% of the noise.

    Idea: a filter that silently returns nothing looks exactly like a quiet star. Every way the data can come in (each product, spacing and era) needs a test that hides a known dip and fails loudly if it does not come back.

    You could try it: research/deep-review-2026-10-23/our-checks/verify_b1_b2.py prints the four spacings and which column the loader picks for each QLP file we have.

    Midnight: the first real search, machine only¶

    You approved a trial on 50 stars: the 5 known planet hosts as controls and 45 others spread by brightness. You asked for the machine-only version first. I will only ask you to look with your own eyes once you have practised Eyes mode and can pass a test on it.

    Nothing real is published from this run: the stars and times of every event stay on this computer, and only counts appear here.

    • There are fewer real dips than fake ones. Per star-sector the search finds 0.08 real events and 0.18 upside-down events. So the upside-down count over-estimates false alarms (flares and scattered light make more bumps than dips), and nothing in this sample shows an overall excess of real dips.
    • Two thirds of the events sit within half a day of a data gap or a thruster firing. They are flagged for lower priority, not deleted, because a real planet can sit there: two of the four TOI-1338 b crossings did.
    • The automatic checks shrink the list a lot. Of 36 real events at our bar, 12 are left after the gap and thruster flags, 7 after removing moments when other stars also dip and events that also appear upside-down, and 4 after removing events that sit on the star's own eclipse.
    • A second data product agrees with a real dip about 9 times in 10 (91%, for strong fake dips we hid in both), but with only about half the false alarms (49%). So it removes about half the false alarms and costs about 9% of strong dips. Weak dips would lose more; we have not measured that.
    • The eclipse-phase check caught two of five leads sitting on the star's own eclipse, which the automatic mask had missed on noisy, variable stars. It removes about the same share of fake events (19%) as real ones (17%), so it is a safety net, not a magic filter.
    • The controls behaved. On the five known hosts no real planet crossing is expected. Two events passed the flags: one a junk ramp in old QLP flux, one on Kepler-453 with the known planet's depth but a quarter of its length, sitting 0.1 days from where the binary's second eclipse should be. Most likely the binary, not the planet.
    • Projected to all 549 stars: about 60 first-tier events for a person to look at (uncertain by about a factor of two), plus a lower tier of about 350 flagged ones.
    • Three leads survived every check. One is weak. Two look clean but come from stars that only have QLP data, so a second product cannot confirm them. They are leads, not candidates: they need a look at the pixels and the neighbours, and nothing is announced.

    Idea: a control is a star where you already know the answer. Any event there is a false alarm until it is proved otherwise.

    Idea: a check that removes the same share of real and fake events is a safety net, not a filter. Know which one you are holding.

    Night: an outside brief on how to show you the dots, and what it taught us about our own practice¶

    Another Claude, with web access, wrote a research brief on how a page should present a candidate dip to a beginner and to an AI without one anchoring the other. I did not build from it. I checked it first (our Compass review), and I read the original papers for its key claims.

    • What held up: hiding the star's name until after the answer, hidden fake dips with known answers, an equal-sized "unclear" button, no planet-shaped model drawn on the data before both answers are in, and scoring "can you see dips" separately from "do you say planet too easily". I read the Planet Hunters TESS and Space Warps papers and the TESS vetting paper: the quoted rates (30% fake dips at the start, falling to 1%, or never below 10% in Space Warps) and the "undecided" option are really there. The medical result that a wrong AI hint can drag even experts down to about 45% accuracy is reported consistently, but I only read news coverage of it, so I call it likely, not proven.
    • What would have been a mistake: the lineup test. The idea is to hide the real dip among a few empty stretches and see if you can pick it out. I tried it in a simulation of pure noise with no planet at all. If the empty stretches are random, a perfect observer still picks "the dip" 84% of the time, because the dip was chosen as the biggest wiggle of the whole sector and a random stretch is not. If the empty stretches are the biggest wiggle found by the same search on other noise, the observer drops to 20%, which is pure chance, as it should be. The comparison is only fair when both sides were picked the same way.
    • What it showed about our own practice: your "quiet" practice curves are random stretches. On real data you will only ever see the machine's best-looking wiggle in each sector. So your false-alarm score on easy quiet curves understates how often you would be fooled for real. The fix is a new kind of practice card, "noise at its best", built from the detector's top event on upside-down or noise-only data.
    • Our pass line, measured: I simulated readers of known skill. Someone who truly finds 75% of dips and false-alarms 12% of the time meets the line in the first two rounds about 9% of the time; someone at 90% and 8% meets it only 55% of the time. So the line is a fair gate but a noisy one, and that is why I judge the numbers and do not just read the line.
    • What it changes: the current Eyes practice teaches shapes, but it is a clean 36 hour cutout of one star. The real page will show a whole sector, a zoom, where the dip falls in the stars' own eclipse cycle and a second data product, on many stars with different noise. A new layout needs its own readiness test, so a rehearsal with hidden fakes in the real layout is now required before any real lead, not optional.
    • Nothing was built. This was a review. The full verdict is in research/compass-reviews/2026-10-24-vetting-page-research.md.

    Idea: a test is only fair if the thing you check and the thing you compare it with were chosen the same way. Picking the best-looking stretch of the data and comparing it with an ordinary stretch proves nothing.

    Idea: before you ask whether you are good at spotting something, ask whether your practice material is as hard as the real material. Easy practice gives a flattering score.

    You could try: open research/vetting-page-research-2026-10-23/our_checks.py and change the lineup simulation so the empty stretches are random again; watch the 20% turn into 84%.

    Night: your first official test, 11 rounds in 25 minutes¶

    You did 11 official test rounds and one practice round in about 25 minutes, and the cloud copy arrived by itself. I read them without you sending anything.

    • The numbers: clear dips found 102 of 110 (93%, 95% range 86% to 96%). Faintest dips 37 of 44 (84%). Quiet curves called a dip 10 of 88 (11%). Eclipses, spikes and jumps named correctly 84 of 88 (95%).
    • The learning curve is the real story. In the first 6 rounds you called 10 of 48 quiet curves a dip (21%). In the last 5 rounds it was 0 of 40, and 50 of 50 clear dips found. Your d-prime (how well you tell dips from not-dips, where 0 is guessing and about 3 is excellent) went from 2.1 to 3.7, and your criterion stayed near 0, so you are not just saying "dip" more or less often.
    • Where the early misses were: 9 of the first 12 misses were deep, long fake dips (4,000 ppm, 6 to 8 hours) that you called "odd", taking them for eclipses. By round 4 that mistake was gone.
    • Your wrong answers were slower (median 2.2 s against 1.1 s for right ones). Hesitating on the ones you get wrong is a good sign.
    • What the numbers cannot say: the fake dips come from one generator on one star, as clean boxes of three fixed depths. Real dips have soft edges, and the real TOI-1338 b card was the same transit every round, so from round 5 you answered it in under a second. You also never pressed "Not sure" in 330 curves and never looked back. On real data "not sure" will often be the honest answer, so that needs practice.
    • The day rule stays, but it is small. The pass line asks for 2 different days, not 5. The rounds you did count as one day, because they all fell on the same calendar date. Why not let volume replace the second day: within one sitting you went from 21% false alarms to 0%, which shows how fast you learn, and also that one sitting cannot separate "learned the trick in the last 20 minutes" from "keeps it tomorrow". One cold round on another date answers that, and it takes about a minute.

    Idea: a score that climbs steeply inside one session is good news about the learner and bad news about the test. Ask what changed between the first round and the last.

    Idea: speed can mean skill or it can mean a memorised look. The way to tell them apart is to change the look and see whether the speed survives.

    Questions¶

    1. Why do bigger dots mean brighter stars on the map?

    Magnitude is backwards: a smaller number is brighter. The map turns that into a dot size, so the brightest stars (like Deneb) are drawn largest.

    2. Why does the caption say 546 drawn, not 549?

    Three of our stars have no sky position in the Villanova Kepler catalogue, and we only draw what we can place. The book says so rather than rounding up.

    3. Why did a dip that was obvious at 10-minute spacing vanish at 30-minute spacing?

    The detector only trusted boxes that were at least 80% full of 10-minute bins. At 30-minute spacing two bins in three are empty, so no box ever qualified and the search returned nothing, with no error.

    4. Seven stars said the noise was 1.76 times ideal; all 549 said about 2.5. Why trust the second number?

    The first seven were brighter than most and had the best data. The 549 are the whole list, including faint stars where noise is worse. A sample only describes the population it was drawn from.

    5. Why do we flag events near data gaps instead of deleting them?

    Because a real planet can sit there. Two of the four TOI-1338 b crossings we re-found were within half a day of a gap or a thruster firing. Flagged events go to a lower-priority list for a person to check later.

    6. The eclipse-phase check removes 17% of real events and 19% of fake ones. What kind of check is that?

    A safety net, not a filter. It does not separate real from fake (the shares are about equal); it just catches a specific mistake, an eclipse the mask missed, at the price of ignoring the stretches near eclipses.

    7. Why can the look-back button never change an answer?

    The saving code only accepts the next unanswered question of a round that is still open. An old answer is never written again, and the tests try to change one and check that it fails.
