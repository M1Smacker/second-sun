    20 October 2026: testing honestly¶

    Field note · night 1 and morning · about 12 min

    In plain words

    Overnight we built a better planet search and then spent most of our effort trying to break it: hiding fake planets to see which it finds, flipping the data upside down to count false alarms, and running it on a real G1 star. In the morning, a bug in the professionals' tool (STANLEY) was found and worked around, and we measured our search with fake planets that move like real ones. No new planet. But we now know what our search can and can't see.

    What we did, and why¶

    1. Detector version 2. It slides a box of 3 to 15 hours along the light curve and asks "is it darker in here than usual?" (Chapter 8). It re-found TOI-1338 b in 4 of 8 sectors VERIFIED.
    2. Fake planets. We added made-up transits of known sizes and checked which were found: down to about 6 Earth radii VERIFIED.
    3. Upside-down data. Flip the light curve (2 − brightness) and any dip becomes a bump. Whatever the search still "finds" must be noise: 6 fakes in 10 sectors VERIFIED.
    4. A failed clean-up. Throwing out dips near spacecraft events removed fakes but also half the real crossings. So those became warning flags for a human (Chapter 10).
    5. A timing check. A 2024 paper predicted a TOI-1338 b crossing on 16 December 2025. We found it VERIFIED, 56 ± 5 minutes after the predicted time.
    6. G1 frozen, QLP fixed, auto eclipse finder, Kepler-16 dry run. The one dip on Kepler-16 was the telescope wobbling before a thruster firing. Nothing survived.
    7. STANLEY installed and run (Chapter 12). Its strongest signal was near 96 days (first misread as 94; corrected in the afternoon), close to the real planet's 95.2, but its scores aren't calibrated yet.
    8. Compass reviewed our upgrade plans: REVISE. Its three points: use STANLEY's fake-planet maker instead of building one; odd and even depths only mean something for planets that repeat; centroid checks must predict the expected shift.
    9. The STANLEY bug. Its fake-planet maker placed zero transits. Cause: before adding the transits it ran them through its search scorer, which keeps only transits already showing a dip. Before injection there's no dip, so it kept none. Our wrapper now takes the transit times straight from its orbit simulation.
    10. The realistic test. 6 fake planets, 33 crossings lasting 6.8 to 15.3 hours VERIFIED. Our cleaning kept 90 to 100% of each dip. 6 Earth radii: 8 of 10 found. 8: 10 of 10. 4: none. 3 of 33 crossings were lost inside the stars' eclipses before the search even looked.

    The idea behind it¶

    Injection and recovery is how you measure a search: add signals you know, see what comes back. It turns "we found nothing" into "nothing bigger than X crossed". Without it, a null result means nothing. Chapter 9 is all about it.

    Debugging by stepping through: the STANLEY bug was found by calling its pieces one at a time. Did the orbit calculation give transit times? Yes, 13 to 29. Did they survive the next step? No. The fault sat between those two calls.

    How you could do it yourself¶

    • Lab 4 runs our box search on TOI-1338.
    • Lab 6 hides fake planets of several sizes and asks the search to find them.

    What we found, and what we didn't¶

    • Found: the search's floor (about 6 Earth radii), a 9% hidden loss inside eclipses, two STANLEY bugs, and a prediction confirmed.
    • Didn't: any new planet. G1 hasn't been searched yet, apart from Kepler-16.
    • Mistakes, logged: the first strict clean-up rule; a rule (C7) coded differently from how it was written; a cache that nearly wrote to C:; estimated timestamps written instead of checked ones.

    Questions¶

    1. Why flip the light curve upside down?

    A real dip becomes a bump, so the search can't find real planets in flipped data. Anything it finds there shows how often noise alone fools it.

    2. 3 of 33 fake crossings fell inside eclipses. What does that mean for G1?

    Even a perfect search misses about 1 crossing in 11, because we cut out the stars' eclipses before searching. Our "nothing found" results must count that loss.

    Afternoon: the Bible Overhaul begins¶

    You paused the hunt so the book could catch up. Two lessons came out of the overhaul itself:

    • Labs are real runs. Every command in Labs 0 to 4 was run on your PC before it went in the book, and the "what you should see" boxes are pasted output.
    • Writing a lab caught a mistake. Our 19 October noise number for Kepler-16 (236 ppm per hour) came from a quick method that flattens away slow wobble. Measured the way our search sees the data, it's about 900 ppm; NASA's own file says 542 VERIFIED. Same data, a factor of 4 apart, depending on the method. Chapter 7 teaches why. It's logged in the mistakes register, and it probably explains the early "2 to 5 Earth radii" overstatement.
    3. Why did one star's one-hour noise come out anywhere from 229 to 908 ppm?

    Each method flattens the light curve differently first. The harder you flatten, the more slow wobble you remove, and the smaller the noise looks. Quote the number that matches how your search treats the data.

    Later, batch 3 of the overhaul: writing Lab 8 caught a second slip. STANLEY's strongest orbit is at 96.1 days, not the 93.8 reported overnight. The search log prints the period range high to low, but the results file runs low to high. The real planet's period is 95.2 days, so the conclusion ("close") survives, but the number was wrong for half a day. Lesson: read an array's order from the data file, and check one known feature lines up. Chapter 12 and Chapter 6 are new.

    Batch 4: Chapter 9 (fake planets) and Chapter 11 (pixels and neighbours), with Labs 6 and 7. Two things worth knowing: three different fake-planet tests now agree on a floor of about 6 Earth radii VERIFIED; and TOI-1338's pixels show the 16 December dip on the right star, with the light's centre steady to about a thousandth of an arcsecond VERIFIED. The pixel lab uses a better error bar than our first pixel script (Compass's point): the chance spread of the same measurement at quiet times.

    Batch 5: Chapter 13 (a tour of the project), Chapter 15 (our 15 mistakes, as five lessons), Lab 9 (the whole route on a G1 star of your choice), a rebuilt learning track and a reading list. Writing Lab 9 found a real problem in the G1 list: our rule "morph < 0.5" also lets in stars whose shape was never measured (morph = −1): 136 of 552 rows VERIFIED. The first star I tried was one of them, and it showed no eclipse at all. Not fixed (the hunt is paused); it's a decision for you in the decision log, explained in Chapter 1.

    Batch 6, the stress test (overhaul complete): every one of the 10 lab scripts was run again from scratch and reproduced its page's "what you should see" output exactly. The built site: 40 pages, 3,743 internal links and anchors checked, 0 broken; no long dashes in visible text; no sideways scrolling on any page at phone width (375 px) or desktop width; all 11 lab and chapter pictures load; all 39 pages are in the offline cache; the "Next" links lead through all 25 chapters and labs in one line, with no loops. Your progress page now shows TOI-1338 b's recovery (19 October) and this overhaul as milestones.

    Evening: the quality-filter experiment (you approved it, I ran it)¶

    In plain words

    Our search throws away data that NASA flags as possibly bad. Two known crossings of TOI-1338 b fall in thrown-away data, so we couldn't see them. I tested whether keeping that data helps. The honest answer: it finds those two crossings, but it makes the search worse everywhere else, and it might add false alarms. Separately, the test turned up a surprise about which flux the search reads.

    What we did and why. First I read the files (no experiment yet) and found the catch: in every piece of restored data, NASA's cleaned flux (PDCSAP) is blank; only the raw flux (SAP) exists. So "same flux, looser filter" was impossible. I wrote the plan down before running anything (experiments/quality_mask/SPEC.md): three versions, changing one thing at a time (cleaned + strict filter = our baseline; raw + strict; raw + milder). I rebuilt the baseline from scratch and checked it matched our saved results exactly. Only then did I run the other two.

    The idea behind it (Chapter 8, seventh test): change one thing at a time, and decide how you'll read the result before you see it, so you can't talk yourself into the answer you like. This is called pre-registration.

    What we found (one star, so treat it as a hint): - The milder filter finds both missing crossings, sectors 62 and 30, at about 12 and 10 times the noise VERIFIED. - It lowers the strength of transits elsewhere and hurts fake-planet recovery VERIFIED; false alarms: undecided VERIFIED. - Surprise: raw flux alone, with the same data points, found the published crossing in 7 sectors of 8; the cleaned flux found 4 VERIFIED. The transits are equally deep in both. What differs is the detector's noise estimate, 2 to 3 times larger in the cleaned flux in some sectors VERIFIED. We don't know why.

    What we didn't find: whether any of this carries over to the G1 stars, or whether the extra false alarms are real (too few events). Nothing in the production detector or the target list was changed. All results, the spec and the code are in experiments/quality_mask/; the Compass review is in research/compass-reviews/2026-10-20-quality-mask.md.

    4. Why write the plan down before running the experiment?

    So the result can't be bent to fit what we hoped. Rules chosen after seeing the data are too easy to set just below the signal.

    5. Raw and cleaned flux show the same transit depth, yet the detector finds the transit only in one. What differs?

    The noise estimate. SNR is depth divided by noise; with a larger noise estimate the same depth gives a smaller SNR.
