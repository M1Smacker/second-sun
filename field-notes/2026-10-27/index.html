    27 October 2026: measure the advice before you take it¶

    Field note · day 9 · about 8 min

    In plain words

    Since the last note, three pieces of outside advice came in: a design brief, a font report and our own plan for the learning path. We did not build any of them on trust. We measured them first, and each time something turned up that reading alone would have missed. We also added a plain score line to Eyes mode and two pages for professional astronomers.

    What we did, and why¶

    1. The design brief was checked with a calculator. Another AI wrote a brief for a new look. I ran it through Compass (my review step). Its "muted brass" text colour measured 2.9 to 1 against our rule of 7 to 1. The brief asked for 60 to 70 characters per line, but a phone at 375 pixels fits only about 35 to 42. The revised brief then failed its own colour rule on two surfaces (2.94 and 2.69 against a minimum of 3). A script found that, not reading.
    2. The font report was checked against the font files. You do not like the current look, so we asked another AI which fonts would suit the book. Its answer was sensible, but it never said which characters each font has. I read the character list inside the four font files the book serves today. All four lack σ, μ, λ, Δ, θ, ≈, ≥, ≤ and →. The book uses σ 38 times, ≈ 28 times and μ 11 times, so on your phone those characters come from the phone's own font, not from Inter. That may be part of why the type feels off.
    3. A font test you can read on your phone. A private page shows a real paragraph of this book in five font pairings, night or day, and checks which symbols each font has. Nothing in the book changes until you choose.
    4. The learning path was reviewed, you said yes, and it is built. The book had three reading orders: chapter numbers, the order the project needed them, and the seven learning levels. Four chapters (3, 13, 14, 15) belonged to no level at all. Now the levels are the one path, starting with the first thing to learn and building up. Chapter 3 joined Level 0, chapters 13 to 15 became a last step after Level 6, and the Journey and By topic buttons are gone. The old journey list is now the story page "How the project unfolded". Chapter numbers stay as permanent labels, so the path reads 1, 2, 3, then 7, 8, then 4, 5, 6. Each chapter shows your level and step, and the next link leads to the next level.
    5. Eyes mode now says your score first. After every practice or test, the top of the finish screen says "You got N of 30 right", then how many were wrong and Not sure, and how often you picked each answer. You no longer open My results to find it.

    6. Two pages for professional readers. For astronomers says in one page what is solid and what is not, and Prior work lists the published searches we know and how deeply we have read each. Both say plainly that we have found no planet.

    7. The redesign is a plan you can look at before anything is built. A private page shows what changes and how, with a phone chapter, a PC chapter and a PC Eyes screen drawn from the real Chapter 2 text, two real claims and a real light curve. Nothing in the live book changed, and nothing is built until you approve it.

    8. The plan was revised after your feedback, and a font was measured for it. You asked for Atkinson against Literata, a quieter note margin and an Eyes layout that scrolls naturally. The new private page has seven boards. We also tested which font should draw σ, ≈ and √. Inter, my first idea, has Greek but not the maths signs. A cut of Noto Sans Math is 1.9 KB and covers everything the book uses, with one more glyph (✗) in a 0.9 KB file. You picked Atkinson and left the other choices to us.

    9. The next hunt stage is prepared but not run. Stage 3 would search all 549 stars. Before it, we wrapped the Stage 2 search so its results go to a folder named after the settings, added the eclipse-phase check to every event, labelled the 237 stars that have only one light curve, and checked the eclipse shape of the 136 stars whose shape was never measured. No star was searched and the settings are not frozen.
    10. Your Eyes results were read, and the page that shows them was found cluttered. Over 25 finished rounds (665 curves; every dip is a fake hidden in real noise, and no real candidate has been shown yet), you found 93% of the clear dips in the standard test, and in the last five rounds you called none of 40 quiet curves a dip. Your misses come in three kinds: faint dips under strength 6.5 (you found 14 of 30), jumps and dips at a gap taken for a dip (12 of 82), and early on, deep dips called "odd" (9 times in the first five rounds, never after). You said the stats are hard to find, and the code shows why: after a hard test the same sentences are printed two or three times. The redesign plan now says one fact in one place, verdict first.
    11. The real redesign started, in two steps. You said start, so the book now uses Atkinson Hyperlegible Next for text and headings, IBM Plex Sans for menus and labels, and two tiny symbol files for σ, μ, Δ, ≈, √, ≥ and 13 other maths and star signs, so every phone draws them the same way. A new test checks that all 31 special characters in the book are drawn by a font the book carries. On a PC window 1120 pixels or wider, a chapter now has a left rail (where you are, the 7-level track and the 15 chapters as two labelled lists), a 540-pixel reading column and a quiet margin that opens only the evidence or glossary note nearest the middle of the screen. A new browser test checks all of that at seven window widths. We also removed two sentences that the hard-test finish screen printed twice. The Eyes layout for PC came next (item 13); the final look pass is not built yet.
    12. The design was reviewed by another AI, and the review changed the results screen. I drew a results screen that said "you catch clear fake planets every time and you rarely cry wolf". The review pointed out that your standard test missed 8 of 110 clear dips and called 10 of 88 quiet curves a dip, 11.4%, above the 10% limit. So the screen now says what the numbers say: "You found 92.7% of the clear dips. False alarms on quiet curves are above the limit of 10%." Every measure shows its count, its target and a bar with the target marked. The 95% confidence interval is named in full and explained once, and a practice result is never called a pass. The weakest spot is worded as a pattern to check, not a proven cause. The older lists sit behind "All the numbers". Margin notes also got a rule: a note that cannot sit within 120 pixels of what it explains is left out of the margin, and the chip in the text still works.
    13. Eyes mode got its PC layout. On a window 1220 pixels or wider, the curve sits on the left with the four answer buttons in one row under it and Next under them, and what the numbers say sits on the right. On a 1366 by 650 screen you now see the curve, the feedback and Next at once. The contents list on the right of this page is hidden to make room. The curve's height follows its own shape (0.55 of its width, never under 170 or over 340 pixels), not a share of the window. The Next slot is reserved from the start, so nothing jumps when the feedback appears; on narrower screens it is pinned to the bottom of the window only once there is a Next to press. After Next, the keyboard focus moves to the first answer instead of vanishing. Scoring, saving and the cloud are untouched. A new browser test checks five window sizes (a phone, a zoomed PC, 1024 by 768, 1366 by 650, 1440 by 900): no overlap, every button at least 44 pixels, the answers in one row where there is room, the focus kept.
    14. The real search of all 549 stars ran. You said start, so the settings were frozen (a fingerprint of the code, the star list and every threshold was written down before any result was read) and the search covered 5,064 star-sectors (every star in every sector TESS watched it) in about 30 minutes, with no errors. It found 604 real dips, of which 186 passed the automatic checks. The honest part: the same search on upside-down copies of the same curves, which cannot hold planets, found 174. So the real-curve count is hardly above the false-alarm count, and nothing in the totals says planets are in there. Looking at the 8 strongest isolated dips, the two strongest turned out to be the sloping edge of a long, deep eclipse of the star pair itself, 0.8 to 1 day from the catalogue eclipse. Our rule drops events within 0.35 days of an eclipse, and those eclipses are wider than that. One star showed 33 dips in a regular pattern repeating every 9.06 days in every sector, which looks like another eclipsing pair sharing the same pixels. The other dips were slow drifts that only one of the three data products showed. Not one is a candidate. The list of events stays on the PC and only totals are published.
    15. We fixed what the first search showed, and searched again. Two new flags, both set from physics and applied to the real and the upside-down curves alike. First: a dip is flagged if it touches an eclipse of the star pair, with the eclipse length worked out from the star's size and its orbit (the old rule used a fixed 0.35 days, too short for big stars). Second: a star-sector with three or more dips is flagged as a variable star or a second eclipsing pair, because a planet circling a pair that orbits in 5 days or more needs at least about 15 days per orbit, so at most two transits fit in a 27 day sector. The search itself was unchanged (the second run gave the identical 2,642 events), so only the flags moved. Three of the eight events we had looked at, including the two strongest, are now flagged as touching an eclipse; the other five still pass. The honest result: the real dips that pass fell from 186 to 64, but the upside-down control fell from 174 to 90, so real is still not above the control. One caution: I chose these rules after seeing the first results, so this version is now frozen and must not be tuned further on these stars. We also checked catalogues: 6 of our stars are TESS planet candidates or known planet hosts, 53 are in the TESS eclipsing binary catalogue, and a sixth known circumbinary host (Kepler-1660) is in our list that we have not used as a control. Literature points to one big missing check: a flat baseline test (a transit is a box, a ramp is not). Full notes are in research/stage3/learnings-2026-10-27.md.
    16. We added a flat-baseline test, and the picture got clearer. A planet crossing a star makes a box: the light just before and just after it sits at the normal level. A ramp or the edge of an eclipse does not, and a dip with no data on one side cannot be checked at all. So the search now flags any dip whose flux before or after is off by more than half its depth, or that lacks data on a side. We measured the cost first by injecting fake box dips into 160 random star-sectors: 97.7% of them were kept. The search itself again returned the identical 2,642 events; only flags changed. The results: the real dips that pass fell from 64 to 15 (186 in the first run), and the upside-down control fell from 90 to 38 (174 at first). Real is still below the control at every signal-to-noise bar from 7 to 12, so there is no sign of planets at this detection level. The surprise: more than half of everything the detector finds sits at the edge of a data segment (the start of an orbit or next to a gap), which is where the false alarms live. Of the 8 strongest events we had looked at, 6 are now flagged and 2 remain (a weak dip and a messy drift); neither is a candidate. The honest conclusion for now: the next gain is in how we handle noise (a correlated-noise correction to the signal-to-noise), not in more flags. The fix queue for the next version is in research/stage3/FIX-QUEUE.md.
    17. We labelled the leftovers and measured what the filters cost. None of the 15 real dips that pass all checks sits on a star that TESS already lists as a planet candidate or that is a known planet host, and none is on a star in the TESS eclipsing binary catalogue. So no catalogue explains them, which does not make them real. Stars with three or more dips in one sector have more uniform dip depths among the real curves (68%) than among the upside-down ones (42%), which probably means real eclipse-like structure, though we did not test that statistically. I also wrote a test for a regular period and then removed it: with thousands of trial periods, any three events fit some period by luck, so it could not tell structure from noise. To know what the filters cost real planets, we injected 1,548 fake dips (boxes and gently sloped ones, 4 to 14 hours long, strong ones) into 160 random star-sectors: 86% survive the eclipse and baseline flags. That is a ceiling, because the other flags and the second-product check remove more, and faint dips were not tested. A sixth known planet host in our list, Kepler-1660, produced no dip that passes the checks.
    18. A fourth version, and a correction to my own diagnosis. I had said our signal-to-noise was probably inflated by correlated noise. Reading the code showed that is wrong: the detector already divides each dip by the measured scatter of the same kind of dip across the whole light curve, which includes correlated noise. What remains is heavy tails (flares, ramps) and noise that changes along a sector. So I replaced that idea with three new flags, with the search itself again unchanged: a dip is flagged if the strongest upside-down dip in the same light curve is at least as strong (a fair local contest with no setting), if it falls within a day after data resumes (this costs 14% of the timeline), or if it touches an eclipse whose width we now take from the TESS eclipsing binary catalogue for 40 stars. Result: the real dips that pass are 12 and the upside-down ones 33, so still nothing above the control. A clue: the strongest dip in a light curve is more often an upside-down one (22% of real dips are beaten by one, against 13% the other way). That fits upward spikes such as flares being bigger than downward ones, which means the upside-down control probably overstates false alarms for dips. So "real is below the control" is expected from noise alone and proves nothing either way; the honest statement is only that no signal is visible. A scrambled control, which does not flip the sign, is the next test.

    The idea behind it¶

    Idea (measure the claim): advice is a claim. Some claims have a number that can contradict them, such as a contrast ratio, a character count or a list of characters in a file. For those, checking with a tool beats reading, because a tool can say no. A contrast ratio compares the brightness of text and its background; 7 to 1 means the lighter is seven times brighter once a small allowance is added to both. Black on white is 21 to 1. A character map is the list of characters a font file can draw; if a character is missing, the device quietly borrows one from another font.

    Idea (an honest label on every claim): the font report marked some facts as verified and some as estimates, and the estimates were true estimates. That is the standard we hold ourselves to: say which is which.

    Idea (decide before you build): the learning-path review ended with three questions for you, not with code. Changing the order of a book changes how you learn, and that is your call.

    Idea (make new code rebuild an old answer): before trusting new code, ask it to reproduce a result you already trust. Our Stage 3 preparation rebuilt the published Stage 2 numbers exactly (36 real events cut to 12, 7, 7, 4, 4, 4 and 1), and a re-run of the five known stars gave identical events. If either had differed, the new code would have been wrong, not the old result. This is called a regression test.

    Idea (a flag is not a deletion): a rule such as "this event sits near a known eclipse" marks the event and moves it to a lower tier. It never throws the event away, because a real planet can cross at an awkward moment.

    Idea (count the kind of mistake, not only the score): "93% right" hides that a missed planet, a false alarm and a mislabelled glitch cost the hunt different things. A miss loses a candidate, a false alarm wastes a check, and a glitch taken for a dip is the one that could fool us. Sort the misses by kind before judging anyone, a person or a program.

    Idea (prove a test can say no): a test that has never failed has not shown it works. After the new layout test passed, we widened the reading column in a scratch copy and the test named every page it broke. Only then did we trust its pass.

    Idea (let the interval choose the word): a rate is an estimate, and the 95% confidence interval is the span of true rates it fits. If the whole span is on the good side of a target, say so. If the span crosses the target, say that too. With 32 quiet curves even zero false alarms cannot show the true rate is under 10%, because the interval still reaches 10.7%. About 35 curves with none are needed. More curves, not a kinder sentence, are what move a result.

    Idea (size a picture by its shape, not by the window): a plot's height is set by its aspect ratio, the height divided by the width. A share of the window makes the same plot tall on one screen and flat on another, so the dips change shape. Fix the ratio, then add a floor and a ceiling so it is never too small to read or too big to see whole.

    Idea (reserve the space before you need it): if a button appears only after an answer, leave its slot empty from the start. Then the page does not jump under your finger when it arrives.

    Idea (one fact, one place): a results page is clear when each number appears once, next to the line it is judged against. Repeating a fact in three lists does not make it safer, it only hides the one number you came for.

    What we found, and what we did not¶

    • Found: the book's fonts have no Greek or math symbols (checked by reading the files). Two of the three font pairings in the report also lack Greek in the version Google serves. Four chapters sit outside every learning level.
    • Decided: on the test page you chose Atkinson Hyperlegible Next at the smallest size. It has no Greek letters in the version Google serves, so the redesign must add a small Greek and math set (σ, μ, Δ, ≈ and a few more) and test it.
    • Not found out: whether the fonts are why you dislike the look. It could also be the bright text on dark, the crowded layout on a PC, or both. The font test page is how we separate those.
    • Not tested: the font page has been checked for script errors but not yet looked at on your phone. The new score line is covered by tests on a local copy of the book, not on your device.
    • Found (hunt): the eclipse-phase rule never covers more than 28% of any star's timeline, because the shortest binary period is 5 days. Of the 136 stars with an unmeasured eclipse shape, 80 show a clear eclipse and 56 do not; those 56 would be searched last.
    • Not decided: the Stage 3 plan mentions a search for pairs of dips, but nothing for it is built. It needs your decision before the run.
    • Found (Eyes): in the first standard sitting your score went from 77% in the first two test rounds to 90% or more in every test round from the fourth on, and the last third of a round scores as well as the middle (83%) and above the first third (78%), so there is no sign of tiredness. Dips at strength 6.5 and above were found 84% to 98% of the time; below 6.5 only 47%, which is roughly under the search's own cut of 7, so that is the limit of the data and not a flaw in you.
    • Not tested (Eyes): every dip so far is a fake. The standard test and the hard test each still need a second day to count, and the real-candidate mode has had no rounds yet. Two rounds on a second device are too few to compare it with the first.
    • Not tested (F4): the new fonts, the PC chapter layout and the Eyes layout were measured by script and looked at in one window (the Eyes layout at five sizes), not yet on your iPhone or your own PC. A window between 1100 and 1120 pixels still gets the phone layout, a little later than the 1100 we drew.
    • The real search ran (item 14), once, with settings frozen first. Nothing in it is a planet candidate yet, and no event has been checked by a human.

    Questions¶

    1. A font file lacks the character σ. What does your phone do when a page asks for it?

    It quietly draws σ from another font on the phone. Nothing breaks, but the letter can look slightly different from the text around it.

    2. Why was it better to calculate the contrast of the brass colour than to look at it?

    A calculation gives a number that can contradict the claim (2.9 against a rule of 7). Looking only gives an impression, and our eyes forgive low contrast on a bright screen.

    3. Why did we re-run the five known stars through the new code before searching the rest?

    To prove the new code returns the same events as Stage 2. Then any difference in Stage 3 comes from the new rules, not from a slip in the code.

    4. Why is a single score such as 93% not enough to judge how well someone spots dips?

    Because the wrong answers differ in kind. Missing a faint dip, calling a quiet curve a dip and mistaking a jump for a dip each cost the hunt something different, so the misses must be sorted by kind first.
