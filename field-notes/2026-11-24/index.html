    24 November 2026: the lists run out, the light does not¶

    Field note · day 37 · about 3 min

    In plain words

    We put every public list of eclipsing binaries together and counted what is left for us. The answer: very few long-period pairs that we have not searched already. The big pool is a list of 10,531 newly found bright pairs, and most of them have no known period at all. So the Census's next step is to find periods ourselves, from TESS light.

    The lists, merged¶

    List Pairs What it gives
    Prsa et al. 2022 (TESS) 4,584 period, eclipse time, eclipse widths
    TESS Ten Thousand, new pairs 7,936 period, eclipse time, eclipse lengths in hours
    TESS Ten Thousand, known pairs 2,065 period and eclipse time only
    CALNet 10,531 which stars are pairs; a Gaia period for 1,559

    Together: 24,902 stars. A check that could have failed: our reading of the rule that picked the G2 hunt list rebuilt that list exactly, 895 of 895 stars.

    What is left Stars
    Not searched yet, period over 5 days, with eclipse times and widths, bright enough 582
    ...with a period over 10 days 21
    CALNet pairs with no period anywhere 8,814

    The lesson: the catalogues were built for other jobs, and the slow pairs a planet hunt wants are exactly the ones they time worst (yesterday: Gaia's periods for slow pairs are mostly wrong). The new work is a period finder for one star's TESS light, checked first on 150 pairs whose answers are known. It is written for Codex, with its pass marks fixed before it runs.

    Also today¶

    • Lessons 4.1, 4.2 and 4.4 corrected before anyone read them: an outside check (Codex) found five teaching errors, each re-derived by our own script first (FIX 103).
    • New lesson 4.3, Measuring stars from their eclipses. Both eclipses of our two-sun pair give the same minimum size, 2.35 Sun radii, which shows the orbit is almost exactly edge-on. The paper's 2.70 is 15% larger, most likely because a straight-sided fit misses the faint first and last moments of each eclipse.
    • Lesson 4.5, The stability limit, and why they are rare, completes Unit 4. A planet around two stars must orbit outside an edge a few times the stars' own orbit; ours sits 1.34 times outside it. Kepler found none around pairs faster than 7 days, which is why our hunts skip them.
    • Lesson 5.1, Telescopes: collecting light, completes Unit 5. Our star gives TESS 2.55 million electrons every 2 minutes; its measured noise shrinks with averaging exactly as photon noise does, at about 1.4 times the photon limit.
    • Lesson 5.2, How TESS watches the sky. One real sector of our star, with the hole where TESS sends its data home (2.74 days of usable light lost, 11% of the sector), and the cameras, cadences and hemispheres measured from our own copies of TESS's lists.
    • Lesson 5.3, From pixels to light curves. How 20 pixels become one number every 2 minutes, and the two corrections in every TESS file; our file's numbers agree with the published correction rule to 0.001%.
    • Lesson 5.4, What goes wrong. The quality flags of one real sector: 14.5% of the measurements set aside, a hole while TESS points at Earth, scattered light raising the sky twelvefold, thrusters about once a week. This is where Petrova's false alarms live.
    • Lesson 6.1, Smoothing out slow changes, opens Unit 6. A running median eats a dip as long as its window: on our star's 13-hour eclipses, 17% of the depth survives a 6-hour window and all of it a 2-day one. Its first check failed, and for a good reason: in sector 46 the deep eclipse fell inside TESS's data-sending hole, and the script had assumed the deepest visible dip was it.
    • A second outside check finished (Codex): every main number in lessons 3.4, 4.1, 4.2 and 4.4 agrees with its own independent measurement; four sentences now say more exactly what they assume, and answers typed as fractions are accepted (FIX 104).
    • The D1 test is running again after yesterday's safe stop, from its saved checkpoints.
