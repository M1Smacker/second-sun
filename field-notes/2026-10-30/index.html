    30 October 2026: we tried to make the filter better and learned why it will not be free¶

    Field note · day 12 · about 6 min

    In plain words

    Since 29 October we have been asking one question: how can our filter find smaller circumbinary planets? Today we finished a long round of tests. Every idea we tried either found more fake dips as fast as it found more real ones, or found nothing new. That is a result, not a failure of effort. It tells us the filter is close to a balance: more planets caught costs more false alarms, about one for one. The choice of how many false alarms to accept is yours, not a thing to tune quietly.

    What we did, and why¶

    1. We asked whether the next big step (stacking) was worth it. Stacking means adding up several transits of the same planet to see a smaller one. Before doing any work we wrote a rule down: skip it if the known planets are mostly slow and stacking reaches few of our stars. We read the NASA Exoplanet Archive: 14 transiting circumbinary planets are known, 11 of them have periods over 100 days, and the middle one is about 194 days. At that period stacking reaches only about 10% (first star list) to 19% (second list) of our stars, even in the best case. The rule said skip, so we skipped.
    2. We looked at the false alarms that get through. They are not white noise. Our nine checks stop about 98% of upside-down events, at every brightness; the 2% that get through look like short glitches. On both star lists the 3 hour box held most of them: 14 of 32 and 16 of 25.
    3. We read how a published search handles the same problem (the mono-cbp paper, Davies and others, 2026). It reaches the same conclusion: most things that pass are leftover systematics. Its extra check, that a dip must survive many smoothing settings, costs up to two thirds of the shallow long dips, the same trade we had already measured.
    4. We tested a box floor. The idea: drop the 3 hour box, the false alarms go, we gain room to loosen another check. We wrote the rule and the hope before running anything. Results on both lists: with the box gone, the false alarms did not go. They moved to the 4 hour box. The count of short-box survivors stayed at 17 on the first list and went from 20 to 16 on the second. The floor cost 7 to 8 points of recovery for 4 hour dips and bought nothing significant. Combined with the looser step check, false alarms stayed at 10 to 12 per 1,000 star-sectors against our limit of 8.
    5. We filed every result, including the failures, in the plan, the version table and a shopping list for the next version. Nothing was tuned on the results, and nobody opened a real event.

    The idea behind it¶

    Idea (write the rule before the result): the stacking gate and the box-floor test were written down first, so the answer could not bend to what we wished. The gate said skip, and we skipped; the test said no gain, and we recorded no gain.

    Idea (an exchange rate): for every idea that did work, the gain in real dips was about equal to the gain in false alarms. When a filter sits at a balance like that, the right question is not "which trick?" but "how many false alarms are we willing to pay for how many planets?"

    Idea (where something is found is not what causes it): the false alarms were most common in the 3 hour box, so I blamed the box. But when we removed it, the same glitches were found by the next box. A count by box shows where a thing is caught first. To find the cause you must ask: if I remove this, where does it go?

    Idea (check a borrowed rule's conditions): I used a published "smoothing window is three times the transit" rule in a case where its conditions did not hold (our smoothing does not mask the dip). It cost a quarter of an 8 hour dip. Read when a rule applies, not just what it says.

    How you could do it yourself¶

    • Look up the periods yourself on the NASA Exoplanet Archive (filter for circumbinary, transiting) and take the median.
    • Re-run the box-floor test: from the project folder run cd pipeline, then ..\.venv\Scripts\python.exe test_vopts.py (a minute; it prints PASS lines). The full runs take hours at low priority.
    • Read the three tables in research\stage3\VESPER-VERSIONS.md and look at the last column: it is the reason each idea was not accepted.

    What we found, and what we did not¶

    • Found (CONFIRMED, our own runs and the archive): stacking reaches few stars at the periods most known planets have; the false alarms that get through are short glitches; dropping the 3 hour box moves them to the 4 hour box; every option tried trades about one real gain for one false alarm.
    • Found (LIKELY): the filter is near a balance, so only new information (a physical duration limit per star, or repeat transits for the short-period planets) can improve it without a cost.
    • Not tested: a physical duration limit per star (needs star radii, a download that needs your yes); a higher bar only for the shortest boxes; the repeated-transit check for the short-period end; any real event.
    • Uncertain: how many of our binaries can show a 3 hour transit at all.

    Questions¶

    1. Why did we write the stacking rule before reading the archive?

    So the answer could not be shaped by what we hoped for. A rule written first is a test; a rule written after the result is a story.

    2. The 3 hour box held most false alarms. Why did removing it not help?

    The false alarms are short glitches, and the 4 hour box catches the same glitches. The box showed where they were found, not what made them.

    3. What does 'one for one' mean here, and why does it matter?

    For each extra real dip we recover we also let through about one extra false alarm. So there is no free improvement left in tuning; the number of false alarms we accept has to be decided on purpose.

    4. Why is it good news that the idea failed?

    We know why, we filed it so nobody repeats it, and the next step is clear. A clean, measured null is the win condition of this project.

    Later the same day: the v8 design¶

    With the testing round finished, we wrote the next version. Three choices were open (how many false alarms to accept, whether to keep the shortest box, how to weigh the dip lengths). For each one I kept the rule we had written before the tests, because changing a rule after seeing which options fall just short of it is a quiet way of tuning on results. You can still change any of them before the freeze.

    So v8 is the old filter plus one new check: the orbit-locked flag. A dip that repeats in another sector at exactly a whole number of the binary's own orbits, with the same length and depth, is most likely a leftover eclipse, not a planet, because a planet keeps its own clock. On the development stars it removes 49 events on 7 stars, the real-minus-control excess drops from +65 to +20, and it costs about 0.8% of planted fake planets. On the first star list it changes nothing. The check is built, tested and gives exactly the numbers we measured offline. It is not frozen yet: the freeze and the sealed stars wait for your go.

    5. Why is a dip that repeats at a whole number of binary orbits suspicious?

    The binary's eclipses keep the binary's clock. A planet orbits more slowly on its own clock, so its transits do not land on whole numbers of binary orbits except by chance.

    Later still: you approved, and v8 is now frozen (Vesper 0.8.0, fingerprint 5f73692944aa). Before freezing we re-ran the old checks with the new flag on: 300 of 300 sample star-sectors give exactly the same events and tiers on the first star list, and all old tests pass. One old test failed first: its made-up events had no depth, which every real event has. The fix was to the test, not the filter.

    Evening: someone else did the careful part, and we read it¶

    Ahmed found a news story: an independent researcher, Pavel Rabtsevich, used AI agents on 126,000 TESS stars and published a possible planet (1.4 Earth radii, every 3.18 days) around TIC 4206066. We read the preprint record and two news reports before saying anything. The planet is not confirmed: TESS watches the star from 31 October to 26 November, and the result does not exist yet. The professionals' pipelines had also seen the signal earlier. What stands out is his habits: rules and dip times written first, 74 attempts to break his own result, public data and code, and "no statistical validation is claimed". Those are the habits this project already has. The full note is research/stage3/rabtsevich-literature-2026-10-30.md, and Chapter 14 has a short section on it.

    Night: a window onto the search¶

    Ahmed asked to watch the search happen. We built a Search window page in the book. While a run goes, a small script counts what the search has written (stars done, dips stopped by each safety check, the self-test of fake planets, files skipped) and publishes only those counts. It never shows a real dip. For the one-time sealed search it runs in blind mode: real and upside-down dips are pooled, so nobody sees the answer before the analysis we wrote down in advance. Tested on the finished development run (3,425 star-sectors) and on a partial copy for the "running" state. Honest limit: I do not look at stars by eye; every star gets the same checks, and the page lets you watch them work.

    Later that night Ahmed asked that the page update by itself, show whether the search is working, and say what is happening. The page now has a live light (running, no process found, not updating, finished), a path diagram where the working step glows, speed charts, counts by dip length and strength, a notes line from Claude, and a collapsed "for the curious" panel with the technical settings. It reads its numbers from a separate data branch on GitHub every minute, so nothing needs a tap and the book does not have to be published again for each update. We tested it with a fake run that grew while the page stayed open: the numbers changed with no reload.

    Ahmed then allowed pictures of real stars and real dips on the Search window so he can learn from them. The page now draws real TESS light, 36 hours either side of an event, with the dip marked and the data gaps dashed, and says in one sentence why a stopped dip was stopped. In the sealed run the pictures are blind: real and upside-down events are mixed, the kind is not even stored in the exported file, and the labels are revealed only in the analysis we wrote down in advance. Still unpublished: event tables, candidate claims, any verdict. He also asked us to cut generic lines from the page, which is now a rule for new pages.

    Late night: getting the sealed run ready¶

    Ahmed approved the next step, and said not to stop before the download. Before any sealed file was fetched we wrote the permission into the locked rules as Amendment 1, so the order of events is on record: rules, freeze, approval, download. Then we built four things that touch no result. A runner that refuses to start unless the code on disk has exactly the frozen fingerprint (we tested it with the wrong switch, the wrong star set and the right one). A scorecard that prints the eight benchmark lines with their pass marks written beforehand; we tried it on the development stars, where it shows why the new orbit-locked check matters: without it the development stars look like an excess, with it they do not. The expected transits of the known planet TOI-1338 b, taken from the discovery paper's prediction table and checked cell by cell against the page, so that later we can say which of its transits our search should have caught. And a download script for the 594 sealed stars, now running in the background. The search itself has not started and waits for a separate yes.

    The sealed search: what it found (30 Oct, night)¶

    Plain answer: we found nothing. Vesper 0.8.0 searched the 594 sealed stars once (6,877 star-sectors, about 50 minutes of search). The real light kept fewer dips than the upside-down copy: 26 real against 59 upside-down at the 7 bar, where noise alone predicted about 42 real. Under the rule we wrote before the run that is NO EXCESS: a measured null, not a detection.

    The scorecard in short: S1 no excess. S2 false alarms 8.91 per 1,000 against a mark of 8, so FAIL on the mark, though the 95% interval (6.80 to 11.47) contains 8. S3 pass (planted dips 37.5%, planted bumps 37.3%). S4 completeness of planted dips: 41% at the 4 hour box, 47% at 8, 34% at 14, 28% at 20, close to the development slice (only the 20 hour box differs beyond both intervals: 28.0 against 21.3, in the good direction). S5 the orbit-locked flag moved 13 real events on 4 stars to tier 2 for a 0.51% cost. S6 below. S7 labels split filed. S8 all 6,877 star-sectors read.

    Why real sits below upside-down: stars flare upward, and flipped a flare becomes a dip, so the upside-down light has more dips to begin with (1,044 against 741 raw at the 7 bar, ratio 1.41). The noise-expected line uses that ratio.

    TOI-1338 b, the one known circumbinary planet. By the rule written before the run: 1 of the 7 strong tests found (sector 37). After the fact, and NOT part of the test (UNCERTAIN): in five later sectors (65, 89, 93, 96, 98) the search saw real events of signal-to-noise 7 to 19 that sit 0.6 to 1.4 days after the published prediction, the offset mostly growing with time. The prediction comes from a 2020 fit extrapolated years ahead, so a drifting ephemeris could explain it. These are not counted as finds. The check against a later published ephemeris is in the fix queue.

    Two bugs of mine, found while reading. (1) The page code had score bands only up to 1e9. One star-sector had a dip score of 3.5e26 (its noise estimate was near zero), so the page could not read that file, counted 6,876 of 6,877 groups, never said FINISHED, and the runner waited for it and crashed after the search was done. (2) The labels report used the development noise file instead of the sealed one. Neither touched a search result; both are fixed and committed. The 3.5e26 event itself was stopped by two checks (tier 2).

    New: What the search found. The Search window now opens with one plain verdict, a funnel from all dips to leads (real beside upside-down) and a card for each lead. Lesson from building it: my first version counted the first-pass files and showed real above upside-down while the scorecard said no excess. The last checks and counting only the best light curve of each star-sector explain the gap. I rebuilt it from the scorecard's own events and wrote the change into the rules as an amendment, in the open. On the sealed run it shows 6 leads against 25 upside-down copies, and says to treat them as noise until a check says otherwise.
