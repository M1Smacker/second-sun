    28 October 2026: check the rival before you copy it¶

    Field note · day 10 · about 5 min

    In plain words

    You asked for an outside audit of one question: should we build a faster, learning-based search, or keep improving ours and test it against a professional tool? All three passes are done. Pass 1 studied a professional tool called mono-cbp, without running it. The most useful finding was small and countable: our 549 stars and their 512 stars overlap on only 30. Pass 2 studied VarWISE, a machine-learning catalogue, and found it is built for a very different job. Pass 3 weighed the options and recommends improving our own search, with mono-cbp as a small yardstick. Then you said go on v5, so we ran a better version of our own search and measured, for the first time, how faint a dip it can see. Then you said to go ahead with the yardstick test and to build the pair search, so we ran the professional tool on 30 shared stars and linked dips across sectors.

    What we did, and why¶

    1. We read the paper and the code, not a summary of them. The team (Davies and others, 2026) searched 512 eclipsing binaries, 3,808 light curves in all, and found one weak candidate. I checked those numbers against the paper and against the table in their code repository, which holds exactly 591 starting stars before their last cuts.
    2. We checked the licence. The code is GPL-3.0. That is a "share alike" licence: you may use and change it, but a program that includes its code must be shared under the same terms. Our plan says MIT for our code, and no licence file exists yet, so nothing is locked in. The safe route is to run their tool on its own and never copy its code into ours (LIKELY, not legal advice).
    3. We counted the overlap with a script. Only 30 of our 549 stars are in their 591-star table VERIFIED. Our stars are fainter (middle brightness 12.45 against about 10.2 for theirs), and 70 of ours have binary periods under 7 days, which they left out. Of our six known circumbinary planet hosts, only Kepler-1647 is in both lists.
    4. We found that their paper and their code may differ. The paper describes an MCMC fit when it compares a transit with other shapes. The code appears to use a least-squares fit instead (LIKELY: I read the source through a summary, not line by line). If so, "benchmark against mono-cbp" means benchmarking the code, not reproducing the paper's exact numbers.
    5. We fixed one of our own sentences. The page The first real search said "we can search others they did not", which suggests most of the 4,584 are still open. Their 512 are the long-period, well-separated ones, so the rest are mostly close, overlapping or short-period systems. The line now says what is true and names the comparison as the next step.
    6. You set a working rule. Tiny changes such as a wording fix, a claim row or a field note are mine to make. Big things and decisions still come to you first.
    7. Pass 2: we read the VarWISE papers, and the two paper numbers in the audit prompt were swapped. arXiv 2409.15499 is VARnet, the detector (Paz 2024). arXiv 2605.19059 is VarWISE, the catalogue built with it (Paz and others 2026). VarWISE is not a planet search. It lists 1.9 million variable objects found in infrared data from a space telescope called NEOWISE, which watches each patch of sky for a few days about every six months. VARnet was trained on about a million fake light curves and checked on only 231 real objects, 48 of them eclipsing binaries. It is very fast, about 52 millionths of a second per source, because it was built for hundreds of millions of sources. Our whole 549-star search took about 40 minutes of machine time, so speed is not our problem.
    8. Pass 3: we compared three options and wrote the report. Option A is to improve our own search (test faint dips, search longer dips, mask eclipses before flattening). Option B is to run mono-cbp on a small shared set as a yardstick. Option C is to build a learning-based engine like VarWISE. The report recommends A first with B as a small side test, and C only if certain conditions are met later, such as tens of thousands of light curves or a few hundred human-labelled events to learn from. The full report, with the table, the benchmark plan and the cost of each step, is in research/audit-2026-10-28-report.md. Nothing in it is built yet, and the choice is yours.
    9. v5: we searched longer dips and measured how faint a dip we can see. The search now also tries a 20 hour box (the longest before was 15 hours). We first proved that the old settings still reproduce the Stage 2 numbers exactly, then froze the new settings and re-ran all 549 stars (5,082 star-months, no errors). First-tier events: 14 in the real curves against 35 in the upside-down copies (v4 was 12 against 33). The upside-down copy finds about 2.25 times more dips than the real one at our bar, so noise alone would give about 15.6 real events, and we got 14. There is still no sign of a planet. The new measurement: we hid fake dips of graded depth in 240 random star-months. An 8 hour dip is found 61% of the time when its depth is 10 times the scatter of 8 hour averages, 22% at 8 times and 2% at 6 times, and the checks then remove about a fifth of the ones found. So a faint planet could be hiding: below about 8 times that scatter the search is nearly blind.

    10. We corrected our own number. I had called 82.7 to 86.4% the completeness of the search. That was only the share of found dips that pass the checks. Only 72% of the deep fakes were found at all, so the whole chain keeps about 62%. We also measured, on made-up data, that the 2 day smoothing window cuts a 20 hour dip to 57% of its depth (a 15 hour one to 68%). A longer window for long dips is queued as v6. We did not build the per-star false-alarm level: both controls we have fail for single dips, so it would have had no honest null.

    11. We ran the rival tool, on its own, on the same stars. You said go ahead, so mono-cbp now lives in its own folder outside our project, with its own Python environment, so its licence never touches our code. It took three tries to install, because it needs older versions of two maths libraries. Then we fed it our local copies of the 224 star-sectors of the 30 stars both searches share, and also the same files turned upside down. It finished in about 7.5 minutes each time on one core. It found 437 events on the real curves and 209 on the upside-down ones. Ours, on the same stars, found 41 and 46. Only 3 of our 41 events were also found by it, and none of its 144 stronger events were found by us. Those strong events are deep (about 1%) and short (about 4.5 hours), and half sit on three stars, which looks like leftover eclipses of the binaries, not planets. We did not look at any single event. The honest reading: the two tools count different things, so a bigger number is not a better tool.

    12. A known planet is a better judge than a count. Instead of arguing over counts, we gave both tools the same question: do you find the published transits of TOI-1338 b? Our normal input (a cleaned version of the flux called PDCSAP) found 4 of the 8 transits that have data. The same files read as plain SAP flux found 7 of 8 VERIFIED. Either one alone finds all 8, because they fail in different sectors. mono-cbp found 7 of 8, but its own vetting step called 6 of them a sinusoid, not a transit, with the default settings VERIFIED. That led to a wider check on the 224 star-sectors: the noise is about equal on average, but SAP is clearly quieter than PDCSAP in about a quarter of the sectors, and clearly noisier in about one in eight LIKELY. This is the most useful thing the yardstick gave us. You said go, so item 14 below is what we built; it also shows that the quarter does not carry over to the whole campaign.

    13. Pair search, and a print I changed. You said build it, so a first version now links dips of one star that sit in different sectors and have similar depth and length, and runs the same rule on the upside-down copies. Result on the frozen run: none of the 14 clean real dips pair up (the upside-down copy has 2 such pairs, noise alone would give 0.3). My first print looked very different: 10 real pairs against 2. I then changed two things, and I am saying so here. It used every product's version of a dip, but the report judges a dip by its best product; and it counted pairs, but several similar dips on one star make many pairs that are not independent. Both fixes lowered the real count. I saw the higher number first, and that is recorded. The pair search can only get interesting with weaker dips, which needs a new frozen search.

    14. You said go on item 15, so the search now reads both versions of the light. The new version (v6) is the old v5 search, copied unchanged, plus a second pass over the SAP flux of the best SPOC or TESS-SPOC file in 1,420 star-sectors. A dip that both versions find keeps its old record, and a dip only SAP finds is added. We first proved that the old settings still reproduce Stage 2 exactly (74 and 298 events), that the new rules fail when broken on purpose (7 of 7), and that the new reader finds TOI-1338 b as before (4 of 8, 7 of 8, 8 of 8 together). Does it help? A little, for faint dips. We put the same fake dip into both versions of 240 random star-sectors. An 8 hour dip at 7 times the noise was found 10% of the time with PDCSAP, 11% with SAP and 16% with either VERIFIED. The gain sits where SAP is the quieter version, and that was only about 3% of these star-sectors, not the quarter we saw on the 30 stars in item 12. The list got longer, not better. Tier 1 went from 14 real and 35 upside-down to 33 real and 36 upside-down: the SAP half added 19 real and 1 upside-down VERIFIED. That looks like a signal, and we do not trust it. In SAP the raw counts of real and upside-down dips are equal at strength 7 and upside-down is the larger side at strength 10, so there is no raw excess; four of our checks remove a larger share of the upside-down events than of the real ones (the repeater check removes 55% of the upside-down and 25% of the real), while the two eclipse checks hit more real events LIKELY. Sixteen of the 19 sit in four late sectors and 12 share a moment with other stars, which looks like the camera, not a planet (LIKELY; nobody looked at any event). The pair search on the union again finds nothing. We did not tune v6 to make this go away; a fairer version of the cross-star check and a fair control for SAP are queued as v7.

    15. You said do the fair check first, so the control is now fair where it can be, and the SAP list shrank. We built v7, which has two parts and no new search. First, a fairer burst check. The old check asked: do upside-down events on other stars happen at this moment? That is a fair question for an upside-down event and an odd one for a real dip, so it removed more of the control than of the data. The new check asks every event, real or upside-down, the same question of the same pool: do three or more other stars on the same camera have an event of either kind within 0.1 days? In the SAP half it now hits real and upside-down dips in almost equal shares (78% and 75%), where the old check hit 52% and 66% VERIFIED. Second, a physical test. A dip that only the SAP flux finds must also show, at strength 3 or more, in the PDCSAP flux of the same file. A planet should, because it is the same pixels with the camera drift taken out. A camera glitch that PDC corrected should not. Fake dips pass this test 87% of the time (193 of 222) VERIFIED. The result. Tier 1 went from 33 real and 36 upside-down (v6) to 21 and 32. The SAP half went from 19 real and 1 upside-down to 7 and 0 VERIFIED. Is that evidence? No. The control is still not fair for the SAP half. After the camera check and the eclipse checks, 67 real and 41 upside-down dips are left. The repeater and flat-baseline checks then leave 14 and 2, and one more leaves 14 and 0, so the control has nothing left to compare with. Also, the PDCSAP flux shows 19% of the real SAP-only dips and 23% of their upside-down mirror images, so real dips are not confirmed more often than mirrors LIKELY. That test is a filter, not a proof: about 1 dip in 5 made of pure noise passes it by chance. Half of the 14 clean dips (7) pass it, which is more than the one in five overall, but mirror images with only one flag pass it almost as often (9 of 19), so cleaner stretches simply agree more. One mistake of ours, fixed. A line of the v6 report judged all 33 real events against a ratio that was measured only on PDCSAP flux, and it said the chance of this from noise was 0.00. It now covers the PDCSAP half only: 14 real events against 15.6 expected, chance 0.69 VERIFIED. I did not catch it when I wrote v6. While building the new test I also found that the "PDCSAP" curve we search quietly fills gaps from the plain flux, so the new test reads PDCSAP without that fill. Nobody has looked at any event.

    The idea behind it¶

    Idea (compare on the same stars): two searches can only be compared where they looked at the same stars with the same kind of data. Before planning a benchmark, count the stars both sides share. Ours shares 30.

    Idea (an open licence is a rule about code, not about ideas): a licence such as GPL-3.0 controls copying and sharing the code. Running a program on your data, and reading the method in its paper to write your own version, are different acts from pasting its code into yours. When a licence matters, say which of these you are doing.

    Idea (a paper and its code can disagree): a paper tells you what the authors say they did, the code tells you what runs. When a result is going to be a benchmark, read the code too.

    Idea (recovery is two numbers): how often a fake dip is found, times how often a found dip survives the checks. Quote both. A single "completeness" figure hid that only 72% were found at all.

    Idea (a smoothing window is a trade): flattening a light curve removes slow drifts, but it also eats part of any dip that is long compared with the window. A wider window keeps more of the dip and more of the drift. The right width depends on the dip.

    Idea (fast for whose problem?): a tool's speed matters only against the size of your own job. VARnet's speed answers a sky-wide job of hundreds of millions of sources. Ours is 549 stars, so a faster engine would save minutes, while a better test of what we miss could change what we find.

    Idea (a benchmark needs an answer key): a test of a planet finder needs planets whose answer is known. Only six of ours are known, and one of those is shared with them, so a fair test will need extra known cases and injected fake planets.

    Idea (a known answer beats an agreement count): two tools that count different things will disagree, and a disagreement does not say who is right. A published planet with known transit times is a fixed answer both can be graded on.

    Idea (two inputs can fail on different days): the cleaned flux and the plain flux of the same star each lost some transits the other kept. Using both and keeping the union is cheap insurance, as long as you record which one saw what.

    Idea (say which number you saw first): if you change a method after seeing a result, write down the first result and why you changed it. A change that lowers a surprising number is fine only if the reasons were true before you looked.

    Idea (a fair control treats a thing and its mirror the same): a control only tells you something if every check is applied to it exactly as it is applied to the data. If one check looks at the mirror images of other stars only, it will treat a real dip and its mirror differently, and the difference looks like a discovery. Give the real event and its mirror the same question and the same pool, and the difference goes away. What stays is the part of the noise that is truly lopsided, and no check can fix that: it needs a different kind of control, or a test that does not need a control, such as asking whether a second version of the same light also shows the dip.

    How you could do it yourself¶

    • Count our stars and their brightness: from the project folder run .venv\Scripts\python.exe -c "import pandas as pd; g=pd.read_csv('targets/g1_targets.csv',comment='#').drop_duplicates('tic'); print(len(g), round(g.tmag.median(),2), (g.p_bin_d<7).sum())". It prints 549, 12.45 and 70.
    • Read the licence yourself: open the mono-cbp repository and its LICENSE file, then the GNU GPL questions page.
    • Read the paper, section by section: the sample cuts, then the vetting steps.
    • Rerun our pair search on the frozen events: from the project folder run cd pipeline, then ..\.venv\Scripts\python.exe pair_search.py. It prints counts only.

    What we found, and what we did not¶

    • Found (VERIFIED): the paper's sample numbers match the paper and the table in the code. The licence is GPL-3.0. The overlap is 30 stars, and Kepler-1647 is the one shared known host.
    • Found (LIKELY): the code and the paper differ on how the shapes are compared; the licence advice above.
    • Found (UNCERTAIN): in the paper's numbers, real light curves gave about 2.3 times as many raw events as upside-down ones, while ours gave fewer real than upside-down. That is my own arithmetic and I do not know the reason yet. Running both on the 30 shared stars would test it.
    • Found (VERIFIED, from the papers): VarWISE uses infrared NEOWISE data with epochs about six months apart, has no planet class (its two eclipsing classes are Algol-type and W UMa-type), and reports no completeness. Its authors say so: completeness is not quantified. Sampled objects labelled eclipsing-detached were 100% eclipsing (262 checked by eye). VARnet reports no false-alarm rate, only precision and recall on 231 curated objects. No code release was found for either.
    • Found (LIKELY): a learning-based search for our job would need many labelled planets to learn from. We have six known hosts, and the professional team tested 14. So it would have to train on fake planets only, as VARnet did, and check on a handful of real ones.
    • Found (v5, VERIFIED by our own runs): the old settings still reproduce Stage 2 exactly; the 20 hour box finds 20 hour dips the old grid could not; the real count stays below the control at every strength bar; the faint-dip table above (research/stage3/faint_recovery_v5.txt).
    • Found (LIKELY): planets close to our detection bar are far more likely to be missed than the earlier "1 in 10 still hiding" suggested, because that figure used the keep rate of strong dips.
    • Not tested (v5): faint-dip recovery with every check included (second product, bursts), the same table converted to planet size for a typical star, and a longer smoothing window for long dips. Nobody has looked at any of the 14 first-tier events.
    • Not tested: we did not install or run mono-cbp, and nobody has compared its results with ours. The last 59,000 characters of the VarWISE paper were not read. The decision between improving ours, benchmarking theirs, or building a learning-based search is recommended in pass 3 but not made: it is yours.
    • Found (VERIFIED, our own runs, item 11): mono-cbp 0.1.9 installs and runs on 224 star-sectors in about 7.5 minutes per pass; on the 30 shared stars it finds 437 events on the real curves and 209 on the upside-down ones, against our 41 and 46. Update to the UNCERTAIN note above: mono-cbp's real-to-upside-down ratio on these stars is 2.1, close to the paper's 2.3, while ours is 0.9. Its extra real events are deep and short, and I think leftover eclipses (LIKELY, NOT TESTED).
    • Found (VERIFIED, item 12): on TOI-1338 b, our PDCSAP input finds 4 of 8 testable transits, SAP finds 7 of 8, and together 8 of 8. mono-cbp detects 7 of 8 but its default vetting labels 6 of them as sinusoids. SAP is more than 20% quieter than PDCSAP in 23% of the 224 star-sectors (LIKELY for the wider sample: 30 stars only; cause unknown).
    • Found (VERIFIED, item 13): the pair search finds no pair among the 14 clean real dips. No excess over noise in the wider class either (5 real stars with a pair against 11 expected).
    • Not tested (items 11 to 13): the same injected dips in both tools (the fair recovery comparison); mono-cbp with the paper's own data product and settings; a repeat run to prove it reproduces; the pair search with weaker dips and with the check that shorter periods fall in data gaps. The SAP change to our search is not built.
    • Found (VERIFIED, our own runs, item 14): v6 leaves every v5 event unchanged and adds 550 real and 488 upside-down SAP-only dips. The same fake dip is found about 1.3 to 2 times as often by either version as by PDCSAP alone at 6 to 8 times the noise, and almost never added at 10 times. The numbers are in research/stage3/sap_recovery_v6.txt and aggregate_v6.txt.
    • Found (LIKELY, item 14): the 19 extra tier-1 dips are mostly camera effects, not planets, and the upside-down copy is not a fair control for SAP (four of the checks remove a larger share of it, and SAP noise is lopsided the other way). NOT TESTED: the fair cross-star check, a fair control for SAP, and any look at an event.

    • Not tested (item 14): SAP in the 1,599 files that sit in sectors where QLP is the best product; the recovery table with every check included; the second-product rule for SAP against other files. The v5 numbers (14 real against 35 upside-down) stay the reference.

    • Found (VERIFIED, our own runs, item 15): the common-moment check treats real and upside-down dips alike in the SAP half (78% against 75%, p = 0.27). With it and the PDCSAP test, tier 1 is 21 real against 32 upside-down, and the SAP half adds 7 real and 0 upside-down. The PDCSAP half stays at 14 real against 32 upside-down (v6: 35). Numbers in research/stage3/sap_fairness_fd788be04650.txt and aggregate_v7.txt.

    • Found (LIKELY, item 15): the 7 SAP-only first-tier dips are SAP camera effects, not planets. The upside-down copy runs out after the repeater and flat-baseline checks, so it cannot serve as a null for the SAP half, and the PDCSAP flux does not confirm real dips more often than mirrors (19% against 23%). Three of the 7 sit in sectors 76 and 80 (16 of the earlier 19 sat in sectors 75, 76, 80 and 82). Pair search on the new list: nothing.
    • Not tested (item 15): how many true dips survive the whole chain in SAP (the recovery table with every check), SAP in the 1,599 files that sit in sectors where QLP is the best product, a control that keeps SAP's lopsided noise, and any look at an event. The v5 numbers (14 real against 35 upside-down) stay the reference; v7 gives 14 against 32 for the same half.

    Questions¶

    1. Why did we count shared stars before planning a benchmark?

    A comparison is only fair where both searches looked at the same stars with the same data. With 30 shared stars, the test is small, and that shapes what we can conclude.

    2. Running a GPL program on your data is not the same as copying its code into yours. Why does that matter for our licence plan?

    The share-alike rule bites when code is combined into another program. Running their tool separately and citing it keeps our own code free to be MIT. This is likely but not legal advice.

    3. A tool takes about 50 millionths of a second per star. Why does that not make it the better choice for 549 stars?

    Speed matters against the size of the job. Ours already finishes in about 40 minutes, so the limit on what we find is how well we test and vet events, not how fast we compute.

    4. A fake dip is found 61% of the time, and 80% of the ones found pass the checks. What share does the search keep?

    About 49% (0.61 times 0.80). Quote both numbers, because a figure that only counts the dips that were found hides how many were never found.

    5. A paper says one method, the code uses another. What do you do before building a benchmark?

    Read the code too, then decide whether you are testing the paper's method or the repository's. The two can give different numbers.

    6. mono-cbp found 437 events and we found 41 on the same stars. Why is that not a result about which tool is better?

    The tools count different things: their strength scale is different, they search to much weaker dips, and their strongest events look like leftover eclipses. Only 3 events overlapped. A fixed answer, like a known planet, is a fairer judge than a count.

    7. Our PDCSAP input found 4 of 8 TOI-1338 b transits and SAP found 7 of 8. What do you do with that?

    Do not swap one for the other: either alone finds all 8 only when combined, because they fail in different sectors. Queue a change to search both and keep the union, record which product saw each event, and re-prove that the old numbers are unchanged before freezing it.

    8. A first print showed 10 real pairs against 2, then a fixed version showed 0 against 2. What should you write down?

    Both numbers, and why the method changed: the first print used the wrong unit (every product, and pairs instead of stars). Say that you saw the higher number first, so a reader can judge whether the change was fair.
