    Glossary¶

    Every technical word in the book, in plain words. Pages link here the first time they use a term.

    Aperture¶

    The group of pixels whose light is added up to make a star's light curve. Choosing it well keeps the star's light in and the neighbours' light out. See Chapter 3.

    Arcsecond¶

    A tiny angle: 1/3,600 of a degree. A TESS pixel covers 21 arcseconds. Written ″; an arcminute (′) is 60 of them. See Chapter 1.

    Binary star¶

    Two stars held together by gravity, each circling their shared centre. Our hunting ground: pairs where a planet might orbit both. See Chapter 4.

    Biweight filter¶

    A way to draw a smooth line through a light curve that ignores dips and outliers, so slow trends can be divided out without erasing a transit. Our detector uses a 2-day one. See Chapter 7.

    BTJD¶

    The TESS clock, in days: Barycentric Julian Date minus 2,457,000. BTJD 4025.6 is 16 December 2025.

    Cadence¶

    One measurement in a light curve, or the time between measurements: 2 minutes for SPOC light curves, 200 seconds for today's full-frame images.

    Candidate¶

    A dip in a light curve that passed our first checks and might be a planet. Not a planet until professionals confirm it.

    CDPP¶

    "Combined Differential Photometric Precision": NASA's estimate of how much a star's brightness wobbles over a given time (like 1 hour), stored in each light-curve file. See Chapter 7.

    Centroid¶

    The centre of a star's light on the camera, like the balance point of its pixels. If it shifts during a dip, the dip may come from a neighbour. See Chapter 10.

    Circumbinary planet¶

    A planet that orbits both stars of a pair. Its dips come at irregular times, which is why standard searches miss them. Our main quest.

    Critical orbit¶

    The closest a planet can orbit a pair of stars and stay stable: usually 2.5 to 3.5 times the stars' separation. See Chapter 4.

    CTOI¶

    Community TESS Object of Interest: a planet candidate found by a team outside NASA, posted on ExoFOP. See Chapter 1.

    Depth¶

    How much of the star's light a dip blocks, as a fraction: depth = (planet radius ÷ star radius)². Usually given in % or ppm.

    Detached binary¶

    Two stars far enough apart that they don't touch or distort each other; their eclipses look like clean, separate dips. Our G1 list keeps only these (Kepler's "morph" value below 0.5).

    Detrending¶

    Removing slow drifts (from the star or the spacecraft) so short dips stand out. We divide by a smooth fitted curve with a 2-day window. See Chapter 8.

    Eclipsing binary¶

    Two stars orbiting each other, lined up so that one passes in front of the other. Makes deep, regular dips.

    ExoFOP¶

    The website where astronomers share follow-up information on TESS planet candidates. Since 2026 it asks for a published paper before accepting a community candidate. See Chapter 14.

    False alarm¶

    A "detection" caused by noise or spacecraft effects instead of a real dip. We count them by searching upside-down data.

    FITS¶

    The standard file format of astronomy since 1981: a header of labels plus tables or images. Every TESS file is FITS. See Lab 2.

    Full-frame image (FFI)¶

    A picture of a TESS camera's whole view, saved every 30 minutes, 10 minutes or 200 seconds depending on the year. Every star in view is in it. See Chapter 3.

    Gaia¶

    The European Space Agency's star-mapping spacecraft. Its catalogue gives positions, distances and colours for over a billion stars. See Chapter 1.

    Injection test¶

    Hiding computer-made planet dips of known size in real data and checking how many the search finds. It measures what we can and can't detect.

    Kepler¶

    NASA's space telescope that stared at one patch of sky from 2009 to 2013 and found thousands of planets and eclipsing binaries. Our G1 stars come from its binary catalogue. See Chapter 1.

    KIC¶

    Kepler Input Catalog number: Kepler's ID for a star, like KIC 12644769 for Kepler-16.

    Light curve¶

    A star's brightness measured again and again over time and drawn as a graph: time along the bottom, brightness up the side.

    Look-elsewhere effect¶

    If you search in thousands of places, a few will show something unusual by chance. A search's false-alarm rate must count all its tries. See Chapter 7.

    Magnitude¶

    Astronomy's brightness scale, where bigger numbers are fainter. 5 magnitudes is a factor of 100. See Chapter 1.

    MAST¶

    The Mikulski Archive for Space Telescopes: NASA's public archive holding all TESS and Kepler data. See Lab 1.

    Matched filter¶

    Sliding a template shaped like the signal you want (for us, a box-shaped dip) along the data and scoring how well each stretch matches.

    Momentum dump¶

    A short thruster firing that TESS uses every few days to stay pointed. It jolts the camera and can fake a dip; it's flagged in the data (quality bit 32). See Lab 2.

    N-body simulation¶

    A computer calculation that moves several objects (like two stars and a planet) step by step under each other's gravity. STANLEY uses one to predict when a two-star planet crosses.

    Parsec¶

    A distance of about 3.26 light years, used by astronomers. Kepler-16 is 75 parsecs away.

    PDCSAP and SAP¶

    Two versions of NASA's light curves. SAP is the raw light summed in the star's pixels; PDCSAP has known spacecraft effects removed.

    Phase fold¶

    Cutting a light curve into pieces one period long and stacking them, so repeating events (like eclipses) line up. Our eclipse mask finds eclipses this way.

    Photon noise¶

    The natural wobble in counting particles of light: like raindrops in a bucket, a count of N varies by about √N. It's the floor no instrument can beat. See Chapter 7.

    ppm¶

    Parts per million. 1% = 10,000 ppm. Earth crossing the Sun blocks about 84 ppm.

    Precession¶

    The slow turning of a planet's orbit around a pair of stars. It makes transits switch on and off over years. See Chapter 5.

    QLP¶

    MIT's Quick-Look Pipeline: makes light curves from full-frame images for a huge number of stars. All 549 G1 stars have QLP data. See Chapter 3.

    Quality flag¶

    A number stored with each measurement that warns about problems (thruster firing, scattered light...). 0 means fine. See Lab 2.

    RA and Dec¶

    The sky's longitude (Right Ascension) and latitude (Declination). Every star's address. See Chapter 1.

    Radius¶

    Distance from the centre of a star or planet to its edge. We measure planets in Earth radii and stars in Sun radii (the Sun is about 109 Earths wide).

    Red noise¶

    Slow wobbles where neighbouring measurements move together. Unlike random noise, it doesn't shrink much when you average, so it fakes planet-like dips. See Chapter 7.

    Robust spread¶

    A measure of wobble that ignores a few wild points: 1.4826 times the median distance from the median. Our detector uses it everywhere. See Chapter 7.

    Scattered light¶

    Sunlight bounced off Earth or the Moon into TESS's cameras, raising the background. It creates slow ramps and fake dips, usually near the ends of each orbit.

    Secondary eclipse¶

    When the brighter star passes in front of the fainter one: a shallower dip than the primary eclipse, at another point in the binary's orbit.

    Sector¶

    One TESS stretch of about 27 days looking at the same patch of sky.

    SNR¶

    Signal-to-noise ratio: how big a signal is compared with normal wobble. SNR 7 means seven times the normal wobble. Our detection bar is 7.

    SPOC¶

    NASA's Science Processing Operations Center: makes the cleanest TESS light curves, from 2-minute postage stamps. See Chapter 3.

    STANLEY¶

    A search tool by David Martin's group (Martin et al. 2021) that looks for two-star planets by simulating orbits and stacking all their predicted crossings together. Installed on your PC.

    TESS¶

    NASA's Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite, launched in 2018. It photographs most of the sky, one sector at a time. All its data is free.

    TIC¶

    TESS Input Catalog number: TESS's ID for a star, like TIC 299096355 for Kepler-16. See Chapter 1.

    TOI¶

    TESS Object of Interest: a signal NASA's team thinks might be a planet. TOI-1338 became TOI-1338 b once confirmed.

    Transit¶

    A planet passing in front of its star as seen from Earth, which makes a small dip in the light curve.

    Virtual environment¶

    A private copy of Python with its own toolkits, kept inside a project folder so it doesn't clash with anything else. Ours is D:\second-sun\.venv. See Lab 0.

    White noise¶

    Random wobble where each measurement is independent. Averaging N measurements shrinks it by √N. See Chapter 7.
