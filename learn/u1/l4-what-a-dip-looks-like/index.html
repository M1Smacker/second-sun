    What a dip looks like¶

    This is the moment the whole project is about. Below is the planet around TIC 172900988, caught by TESS in sector 21 (January and February 2020) as it crossed in front of one of its two suns.

    Find the dip. Read its lowest points. How long, in hours, does the light stay low?

    The shape of a planet's dip¶

    Lesson 1.2 said a planet blocks the fraction \((R_p / R_\star)^2\) of its star's light, for a star equally bright all over its face and seen on its own. That is roughly the depth at the deepest part. But the planet does not jump onto the star's face. It slides on, crosses, and slides off, and each part leaves its mark on the light:

    Ingress is the slope down: the planet's disc is only partly in front of the star. Once the whole disc is in front, the light stays near its lowest level: the flat bottom (nearly flat: a star's edge is a little darker than its centre, so the bottom is gently rounded too). Egress is the slope back up as the planet leaves. Because a planet is small next to its star, ingress and egress are short and the flat bottom is long. That box-like shape, with short sloping sides, is the signature of a small body crossing a big one.

    Ingress and egress. The start and the end of a crossing, while the planet's disc is only partly in front of the star. Both are Latin: "going in" and "going out".

    Now go back to the eclipse in lesson 1.1. Its bottom is a point, not a flat line: our script finds only 2 of its 10-minute points within 1 percentage point of the lowest one. When two stars of nearly the same size eclipse, one can cover the other completely only for a moment, if at all, so the hidden part grows and shrinks almost without staying still. A V shape usually means two stars; a U or box shape with a flat bottom usually means something much smaller than its star. Planet searches use this difference all the time to throw out eclipsing stars that pretend to be planets.

    V or U. A rule of thumb, not a law: a planet that only grazes the edge of its star also makes a V. Unit 6 shows how searches handle that.

    Seeing a dip through the noise¶

    Look at the plot again. The points outside the dip scatter by a few hundred ppm: our script measures a spread of \(\sigma = 305\) ppm per 30-minute point. A single point 600 ppm low would mean nothing. Yet the dip is plain to see. Why?

    Because it is not one low point. It is many low points in a row. Lesson 1.3 showed that averaging \(N\) points shrinks random noise to \(\sigma / \sqrt{N}\). The dip is a stretch of points that all sit low together, and their average is far below normal, with very little noise left. Astronomers compare the two with one number, the signal-to-noise ratio:

    \[ \text{SNR} = \frac{\text{depth}}{\sigma / \sqrt{N}} \]

    Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). How many times bigger a signal is than the noise left after averaging. Searches usually ask for about 7 or more before they look twice.

    where depth is the average drop of the points inside the dip, \(\sigma\) is the noise of one point, and \(N\) is how many points the dip holds.

    Worked example: how clear is the planet's dip?

    Our script measured this dip (the second one in sector 21) from its 12 points within 3.6 hours of the middle.

    Step 1. Depth. Their average is 2,655 ppm below normal.

    Step 2. Noise of one point. Outside the dip, \(\sigma = 305\) ppm.

    Step 3. Noise of the average of 12 points. \(\sqrt{12} = 3.46\), so \(305 / 3.46 = 88\) ppm.

    Step 4. Divide. \(\text{SNR} = 2{,}655 / 88 = 30\).

    The dip is 30 times bigger than the noise left in its average. That is a strong signal: a search that looks at this stretch of light should not miss it. This planet is big enough that even one low point stands out a little (2,655 / 305 = 8.7). For an Earth-sized planet, with a dip under 100 ppm, a single point would be hopeless, and averaging is everything. (This simple SNR also assumes independent noise and a well-known normal level; red noise from lesson 1.3 makes the real number smaller.)

    The depth also matches lesson 1.2. There we worked out 5,560 ppm for this planet in front of the larger star if the other star were not there, and said the second star's light would roughly halve it. 2,000 to 3,000 ppm is what TESS saw.

    The other crossing¶

    Five days earlier the same planet crossed the other star. Here it is:

    Is this dip deeper or shallower than the first plot? Does its bottom look flat?

    Two dips, 4.9 days apart, from one planet crossing two different suns. With one star, a planet's dips repeat like a clock, every orbit. Around two suns that clock breaks: the stars move while the planet goes round, so the planet meets them at different places each time. The dips change their spacing, their depth and their length. That is what makes planets around two suns so hard to find, and so precious when found. Unit 4 is all about it.

    Your turn, half done: the first crossing

    In the first crossing our script finds a depth of about 1,900 ppm over 14 points. Take the same noise as before, 305 ppm per point. Step 1: \(\sqrt{14} = 3.74\). Finish the other steps and type the SNR.

    Your turn. SNR of the first crossing:

    Worked solution

    Step 2: noise of the average, \(305 / 3.74 = 81.6\) ppm. Step 3: \(\text{SNR} = 1{,}900 / 81.6 = 23.3\).

    The rule: SNR is depth over the noise of the average, \(\sigma / \sqrt{N}\). Still far above 7: a clear detection.

    In a few sentences¶

    A planet's dip has a short slope down (ingress), a flat bottom and a short slope up (egress); two similar stars eclipsing each other usually make a V instead. A dip is seen through noise because many low points in a row average down the noise: the signal-to-noise ratio, depth over \(\sigma / \sqrt{N}\), says how clear it is. Around two suns, a planet's dips do not repeat like a clock, which is why they are hard to find.

    Problems¶

    1. A dip is 2,000 ppm deep and holds 16 points. The noise of one point is 400 ppm. What is its SNR?

    Worked solution

    Noise of the average: \(400 / \sqrt{16} = 400 / 4 = 100\) ppm. SNR: \(2{,}000 / 100 = 20\).

    2. A dip lasts 6 hours. The light curve has one point every 30 minutes. How many points fall inside the dip?

    Worked solution

    \(6 \times 60 = 360\) minutes, and \(360 / 30 = 12\) points.

    3. A dip is 2,500 ppm deep. The star is 1.3 times as wide as the Sun, and no second star dilutes the light. How many times wider than the Earth is the planet?

    Worked solution

    Run lesson 1.2 backwards. Depth as a fraction: \(2{,}500 / 1{,}000{,}000 = 0.0025\). Since depth is \((R_p / R_\star)^2\), the ratio is its square root: \(\sqrt{0.0025} = 0.05\). The star is \(1.3 \times 109.08 = 141.8\) Earths wide, so the planet is \(0.05 \times 141.8 = 7.09\) Earths wide: between Neptune (3.9 Earths) and Saturn (9.4 Earths) in size.

    4. A search only looks twice at dips with SNR of 7 or more. For one star, the noise of the average over a dip's length is 100 ppm. What is the shallowest dip the search will notice?

    Worked solution

    \(\text{SNR} = \text{depth} / \text{noise}\), so \(\text{depth} = \text{SNR} \times \text{noise} = 7 \times 100 = 700\) ppm. Shallower dips mostly slip under the bar, though noise can push a real dip a little above or below it. Measuring exactly what a search can and cannot see is a whole part of this project (Unit 6).

    5. In one word: what is the short slope down at the start of a planet's dip called?

    Worked solution

    Ingress, while the planet's disc moves onto the star. The slope up at the end is egress.
