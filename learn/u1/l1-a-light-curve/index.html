    Unit 1 · A star's light on a graph · Lesson 1.1 · 12 min

    A light curve¶

    Before any idea, look at the real thing. Below is one star's light, measured by a space telescope for 24 days. Move your finger or mouse along it.

    Find the two deep drops. On which days do they happen, and does one go deeper than the other?

    What you are looking at¶

    This picture is called a light curve. It is the simplest thing an astronomer can make from a telescope that stares at one star: how bright the star looked, point after point, as time went on. Time runs from left to right. Brightness runs up and down.

    Light curve. Brightness against time for one star. Everything in this project starts from one.

    The telescope is TESS, a NASA satellite launched in 2018 that watches large patches of sky for about four weeks at a time. One of those four-week stretches is called a sector. This plot is sector 44, taken in October and November 2021.

    Sector. One stretch of about 27 days when TESS points at the same patch of sky. The light in this file runs over 24 days, with a gap of almost 3 days in the middle while the data are sent down to Earth.

    The star is called TIC 172900988. That long number is its entry in the TESS Input Catalog, the telescope's list of stars. You cannot see it with your eyes, but a small telescope would find it easily. And it is not one star. It is two stars, so close together that TESS sees them as one point of light. They circle each other every 19.7 days. Twice in each orbit one star passes in front of the other (first one way round, then the other), and from here we see less light. That is what the deep drops are: eclipses.

    Eclipse. One object passing in front of another and blocking part of its light.

    There is a third thing in this system, too: a planet as big as Jupiter that circles both stars. Astronomers found it in 2021 from this same kind of light curve, taken in an earlier sector. No crossing of it stands out in this plot. You will meet its real dips in lesson 1.4.

    From counts to a percentage¶

    TESS does not measure "brightness" directly. Its camera counts the light that lands in a few pixels around the star, every 2 minutes. For this star the usual count is about 22,437 units each second (the units are electrons freed in the camera by the light, after the pipeline's corrections). That number on its own means little: a brighter star gives a bigger number, a fainter one a smaller number.

    So the first thing anyone does with a light curve is to compare every point with the star's normal light. We call a point's light \(F\) and the star's normal light \(F_{\text{normal}}\). Their ratio, \(F / F_{\text{normal}}\), is 1 when the star looks normal and less than 1 when it looks fainter. Fainter can mean something blocked the light, as in an eclipse, but also a spot on the star, a wobble of the spacecraft or plain noise (lesson 1.3); telling these apart is much of the work. Subtract 1 and multiply by 100, and you have the change in percent:

    \[ \text{change (\%)} = \left( \frac{F}{F_{\text{normal}}} - 1 \right) \times 100 \]

    Normal light. In practice, the middle value (the median) of all points. A few odd points cannot pull it around.

    That is the scale on the plot: 0 means normal, minus 10 means 10% fainter than normal.

    Worked example: how deep is the first eclipse?

    At the bottom of the first drop, TESS counted about 12,316 units each second. Normal is 22,437.

    Step 1. The ratio. \(F / F_{\text{normal}} = 12{,}316 / 22{,}437 = 0.549\). The star pair gave 0.549 of its normal light.

    Step 2. Subtract 1. \(0.549 - 1 = -0.451\). The minus sign says "fainter".

    Step 3. Times 100. \(-0.451 \times 100 = -45.1\%\).

    So at the deepest moment, almost half of the pair's light was hidden. That is a lot: the two stars are nearly the same size, so one can cover most of the other.

    Your turn, half done: the second eclipse

    At the bottom of the second drop the ratio \(F / F_{\text{normal}}\) is 0.642. Step 1 is done for you: the ratio is 0.642. Do steps 2 and 3, then type the change in percent.

    Your turn. The change in percent at the bottom of the second eclipse:

    Worked solution

    Step 2: \(0.642 - 1 = -0.358\). Step 3: \(-0.358 \times 100 = -35.8\%\).

    The rule behind it: compare with normal, then turn the difference into percent. The answer is negative because light was blocked. This eclipse is shallower than the first (36% against 45%). Why the two differ is the subject of lesson 2.3: it depends on which star is hidden and how squarely the two line up.

    One eclipse up close¶

    Zoom into the first drop. Here each point is 10 minutes of light, averaged.

    Touch the plot where the light first starts to fall, then where it is back to normal. How many hours did the eclipse last?

    The shape tells a story. The light does not switch off and on. It slides down as one star moves across the other, reaches a bottom, and slides back up. The shape of that slide holds the sizes of the two stars and the angle we see the orbit from. Later units take that apart, piece by piece.

    In a few sentences¶

    A light curve is a star's brightness against time. We divide every point by the star's normal light so that 1 (or 0%) means normal and anything lower means the star looked fainter. In an eclipse, this pair of stars loses up to 45% of its combined light; a planet blocks far, far less, which is why the next lessons are about small numbers and about noise.

    Problems¶

    1. A star normally gives 20,000 units of light each second. During a dip it gives 19,800. What is the change in percent?

    Worked solution

    Ratio: \(19{,}800 / 20{,}000 = 0.99\). Subtract 1: \(-0.01\). Times 100: \(-1\%\). The star was 1% fainter than normal.

    2. A point on a light curve has \(F / F_{\text{normal}} = 0.97\). What is the change in percent?

    Worked solution

    \((0.97 - 1) \times 100 = -0.03 \times 100 = -3\%\).

    3. TESS takes one measurement of this star every 2 minutes. How many measurements is that in one full day?

    Worked solution

    A day has \(24 \times 60 = 1{,}440\) minutes. One point every 2 minutes gives \(1{,}440 / 2 = 720\) points.

    (This sector has 15,243 good points over 24 days, fewer than \(24 \times 720 = 17{,}280\), mostly because of the almost 3-day gap in the middle while the data are sent to Earth, and partly because some points are flagged as bad and removed.)

    4. On the TESS clock, the middles of the two eclipses (halfway through the time the light stays below 90% of normal) are at day 2507.40 and day 2519.27. How many days apart are they?

    Worked solution

    \(2519.27 - 2507.40 = 11.87\) days.

    Here is a puzzle to keep: the stars take 19.7 days to go round, so you might expect the two eclipses to be half of that, 9.85 days, apart. They are not. The orbit is not a circle but a stretched oval, so one half of it is travelled faster than the other (how uneven the gap looks also depends on the angle we see the oval from). Unit 4 shows how astronomers read that from this one gap.

    5. In one word: what shape would a planet crossing in front of a star make in a light curve?

    Worked solution

    A dip: the light falls a little, stays low while the planet crosses, and comes back. It is the same idea as the eclipse above, only much smaller, because a planet is far smaller than a star.
